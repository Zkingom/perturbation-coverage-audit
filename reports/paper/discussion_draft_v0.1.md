# Discussion (draft v0.1, 2026-10-15)

**Marginal validity hides a systematic blind spot.** Across three Perturb-seq screens, two cell lines and both essential and non-essential genes, split-conformal prediction sets for a strong linear perturbation model were marginally valid yet covered only about half of the 20% strongest perturbations, while covering weaker perturbations almost always. This is not a small-sample artefact: the pattern holds for 1,751–5,683 target genes, for three response models and for k = 20–200 readout genes, and it survives a split-half test in which effect-strength tiers are defined on one half of the cells and coverage judged on the other. For the biological use of uncertainty estimates — prioritising which predicted perturbations to test, or trusting a predicted strong phenotype — this is the regime that matters most, and it is exactly where a marginal guarantee is least informative. Part of the failure is built into the score: for the mean model the score equals effect strength, so a single global quantile must under-cover the largest effects. The point of the study is that the same failure persists for a fitted model and is not repaired by the usual notion of "distribution shift".

**Distance is the wrong axis.** Out-of-distribution work on perturbation prediction emphasises how far a test perturbation is from the training data. Six deployable proxies built on that idea — GO and STRING distances, similarity- and data-quality-based difficulty, and a bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy in the spirit of PRESCRIBE — produced no T5 gain on either confirmation set, and in confounding analyses distance had no residual association with miscoverage for the primary model once effect strength was accounted for. Effect strength, by contrast, remained the dominant predictor in every dataset. We therefore suggest that calibration audits of perturbation models report coverage stratified by effect strength in addition to similarity-based OOD splits; consistent with this, a recent benchmark found that model rankings depend strongly on perturbation effect size and recommended effect-size-stratified evaluation¹¹, although its direction of dependence varied with the metric and it did not address uncertainty quantification.

**The bottleneck is predicting effect strength before the experiment.** An oracle that knows effect strength restores T5 coverage (≈ +47–52 pp under width matching), and removing measurement noise shared between width allocation and assessment reduced this only modestly. The deployable prior-neighbour proxy recovered a pre-registered, statistically supported part of the gap on RPE1 (E1 +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6)), but not on non-essential GWPS genes (E1 +1.0 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 2.1); well powered). The proxy's predictability explains the difference (ρ = 0.43 vs 0.11), and post-confirmation analyses point to network structure: effect strength was spatially autocorrelated on STRING among essential genes but hardly among non-essential ones, and within every dataset the proxy worked better for complex members and highly connected genes. A plausible biological reading is that essential genes act through dense, co-regulated machines (ribosome, RNA processing, transcription initiation), so a gene's effect is predictable from its partners, whereas non-essential genes act more idiosyncratically. We regard this as a hypothesis, not a finding.

**The repair has a cost.** Normalising by ĥ at equal total width moves width from perturbations predicted to be weak to those predicted to be strong. Perturbations with little prior information pay for this: in the lowest STRING-degree tertile, the change in T5 coverage under M1 was negative in all three datasets, with CIs excluding zero on RPE1 and GWPS (RPE1 -14.9 pp (95% CI -22.4 to -7.5); GWPS -3.7 pp (95% CI -5.5 to -1.8); development -5.1 pp (95% CI -15.8 to 5.5)), whereas the gains concentrated in densely connected genes (GWPS, high-degree stratum at fixed cut-points, +10.2 pp (95% CI 6.0 to 14.3)). These group-wise numbers are descriptive and were not corrected for the nine groups examined. A practitioner should not apply the repair uniformly. One natural extension — enabling the repair only where the proxy is expected to be predictive, for example by network degree — is left to future work; we did not construct or test such a rule on the present data, because any rule tuned here would need new data for a valid confirmation.

**Limitations.**
1. *Circularity.* ĥ is built from neighbours' effect strengths, so "low network autocorrelation" and "low ĥ predictability" overlap conceptually and the statement that one explains the other is partly tautological. The information that is not circular is threefold: the split-half reliability of effect strength (≈ 0.99 in all datasets) rules out measurement noise as the reason for low predictability on GWPS; the stratifying effect of network position (degree, complex membership) on predictability and repair is consistent across three datasets; and a cross-dataset gap persists within matched degree strata.
2. *Reliability is not validity.* Split-half reliability measures repeatability across cell halves; components shared by both halves (for example a cell-number-dependent noise floor in the top-gene selection, or batch) count as signal. An illustrative cross-split analysis (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left ĥ's predictability unchanged (GWPS ρ = 0.119) and reliability stayed high after partialling out cell number (GWPS 0.960). However, on GWPS measured effect strength is strongly anti-correlated with cell number (ρ = -0.80), so part of the strong-effect tier there may reflect measurement precision (a noise floor) rather than biological effect size; this cannot be separated from fitness effects on cell yield with the present data (Supplementary Note 1).
3. *Study bias.* STRING degree and CORUM membership partly reflect how intensively a gene has been studied, not only its biological modularity; our analyses cannot separate the two.
4. *Scope of the confirmation.* All three screens come from one laboratory and platform, and two are in K562; most RPE1 targets overlap the development targets, so RPE1 tests generalisation across cell lines for largely the same genes, while GWPS tests generalisation to new genes. Cross-laboratory replication (e.g. in recently published perturbation atlases¹²) is needed.
5. *Models.* We studied linear models, which match or exceed deep perturbation models on these data²; whether deep models show the same failure is untested here. The selected K and d often lay at the upper end of the pre-specified grids; an exploratory grid extension on RPE1 is reported separately.
6. *Choices of score and unit.* Results are for a perturbation-level score on perturbation-specific genes relative to the training centroid; other scores (per-gene intervals, differential-expression calls) may behave differently.

**Conclusion.** Uncertainty statements for perturbation-response predictions should be audited along effect strength, not only distance. Marginal conformal guarantees are reliable on average but systematically miss the strongest perturbations; the fix requires predicting effect strength before the experiment, which prior networks enable for modular, essential-gene programmes but not yet for non-essential genes.
