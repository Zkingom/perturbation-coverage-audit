# Discussion (draft v0.4, 2026-10-15)

**Marginal validity hides a systematic blind spot.** Across three Perturb-seq screens, two cell lines and both an essential-gene library and a set of targets outside it, split-conformal prediction sets for a strong linear perturbation model were marginally valid yet covered only about half of the 20% strongest perturbations, while covering weaker perturbations almost always. This is not a small-sample artefact: the pattern holds for 1,751–5,683 target genes, for three response models and for k = 20–200 readout genes, and it survives a split-half test in which effect-strength tiers are defined on one half of the cells and coverage judged on the other. For the biological use of uncertainty estimates — prioritising which predicted perturbations to test, or trusting a predicted strong phenotype — this is the regime that matters most, and it is exactly where a marginal guarantee is least informative. Part of the failure is built into the score: for the mean model the score equals effect strength, so a single global quantile must under-cover the largest effects. The point of the study is that the same failure persists for a fitted model and is not repaired by the usual notion of "distribution shift".

**Distance-based proxies did not capture this axis.** Out-of-distribution work on perturbation prediction emphasises how far a test perturbation is from the training data. Six deployable proxies built on that idea — GO and STRING distances, similarity- and data-quality-based difficulty, and a bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy in the spirit of PRESCRIBE — produced no T5 gain on either confirmation set, and in confounding analyses distance had no residual association with miscoverage for the primary model once effect strength was accounted for. Effect strength, by contrast, remained the dominant predictor in every dataset. These are six specific proxies, one score and two linear models, so the finding is that distance did not capture the failure axis here, not that distance is irrelevant in general. We nonetheless suggest that calibration audits of perturbation models report coverage stratified by effect strength in addition to similarity-based OOD splits; consistent with this, a recent benchmark found that model rankings depend strongly on perturbation effect size and recommended effect-size-stratified evaluation¹¹, although its direction of dependence varied with the metric and it did not address uncertainty quantification.

**Heterogeneous coverage is heterogeneous scale.** The conformity score is nearly proportional to measured effect strength (coefficient of variation of S1/E_true 0.107, 0.097 and 0.008 across the three datasets; identical by construction for the mean-response model), so a single global quantile cannot be calibrated across perturbations whose residual scale differs by an order of magnitude. A reference method that divides by the measured scale attains T5 coverage of 0.99 and above, but for the mean model this is definitional and on the non-library target set nearly so, and its intervals were narrower than those of M0 rather than width-matched; we therefore read it only as what is attainable when each perturbation's actual scale is known, not as a bound on any repair. The practical implication is a statement about information, not about biology: a deployable method needs scale information before the experiment. Whether prior knowledge supplies it is an open question, and our one attempt answers it only for one proxy family. The prior-neighbour proxy recovered a pre-registered, statistically supported part of the gap on RPE1 (E1 +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6)) but not on the non-library target set (E1 +1.0 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 2.1); power at the observed SE 0.96 for half the development-set effect). The proxy's predictability differs in the same direction (ρ = 0.43 vs 0.11), and post-confirmation analyses are consistent with a role for network structure: effect strength was spatially autocorrelated on STRING among essential genes but hardly among non-essential ones, and within every dataset the proxy worked better for complex members and highly connected genes. A plausible biological reading is that essential genes act through dense, co-regulated machines (ribosome, RNA processing, transcription initiation), so a gene's effect is predictable from its partners, whereas targets outside that library act more idiosyncratically. We regard this as a hypothesis, not a finding.

**The repair has a cost.** Normalising by ĥ at equal total width moves width from perturbations predicted to be weak to those predicted to be strong. Perturbations with little prior information pay for this: in the lowest STRING-degree tertile, the change in T5 coverage under M1 was negative in all three datasets, with CIs excluding zero on RPE1 and non-library target set (RPE1 -14.9 pp (95% CI -22.4 to -7.5); non-library target set -3.7 pp (95% CI -5.5 to -1.8); development -5.1 pp (95% CI -15.8 to 5.5)), whereas the gains concentrated in densely connected genes (non-library target set, high-degree stratum at fixed cut-points, +10.2 pp (95% CI 6.0 to 14.3)). These group-wise numbers are descriptive and were not corrected for the nine groups examined. A practitioner should not apply the repair uniformly. One natural extension — enabling the repair only where the proxy is expected to be predictive, for example by network degree — is left to future work; we did not construct or test such a rule on the present data, because any rule tuned here would need new data for a valid confirmation.

**Limitations.**
1. *Circularity.* ĥ is built from neighbours' effect strengths, so "low network autocorrelation" and "low ĥ predictability" overlap conceptually and the statement that one explains the other is partly tautological. The information that is not circular is threefold: the split-half reliability of effect strength (≈ 0.99 in all datasets) and its cross-split version make noise in the outcome an unlikely sole explanation for the low predictability on the non-library target set, though reliability counts any component shared by both cell halves as signal and therefore cannot exclude it; the stratifying effect of network position (degree, complex membership) on predictability and repair is consistent across three datasets; and a cross-dataset gap persists within matched degree strata.
2. *Reliability is not validity.* Split-half reliability measures repeatability across cell halves; components shared by both halves (for example a cell-number-dependent noise floor in the top-gene selection, or batch) count as signal. An illustrative cross-split analysis (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left ĥ's predictability unchanged (non-library target set, ρ = 0.119) and reliability stayed high after partialling out cell number (non-library target set, 0.960). However, on the non-library target set measured effect strength is strongly anti-correlated with cell number (ρ = -0.80), so part of the strong-effect tier there may reflect measurement precision (a noise floor) rather than biological effect size; this cannot be separated from fitness effects on cell yield with the present data (Supplementary Note 1) On both essential-gene screens, re-tiering perturbations by effect strength corrected for a cell-number-matched null from non-targeting control cells left the failure essentially unchanged (M0 T5 coverage 0.55 and 0.49); on non-library target set the same correction raised T5 coverage from 0.52 to 0.66 and 21% of perturbations did not exceed the null, so on the non-library target set a sizeable part of the apparent failure reflects the precision of low-cell-number measurements. Reporting coverage by noise-corrected effect strength should accompany any such audit.
3. *Study bias.* STRING degree and CORUM membership partly reflect how intensively a gene has been studied, not only its biological modularity; our analyses cannot separate the two.
4. *Selection of evaluation units.* Perturbations with fewer than 30 cells are excluded from evaluation, and these have systematically higher measured effect strength than the included ones (medians 7.9 vs 3.6, 6.6 vs 3.9 and 9.6 vs 3.2; 73–100% above the T5 cut-point of the included units). The audit is therefore performed after truncating the apparently strongest tail. Since measured effect strength also rises as cell number falls, these perturbations are simultaneously the least precisely measured, and the direction of the resulting bias cannot be signed with these data.
5. *Scope of the confirmation.* All three screens come from one laboratory and platform, and two are in K562; most RPE1 targets overlap the development targets, so RPE1 tests generalisation across cell lines for largely the same genes, while non-library target set tests generalisation to new genes. Cross-laboratory replication (e.g. in recently published perturbation atlases¹²) is needed.
5. *Models.* We studied linear models, which match or exceed deep perturbation models on these data²; whether deep models show the same failure is untested here. The selected K and d often lay at the upper end of the pre-specified grids; in an exploratory extension on RPE1 (K up to 200, d up to 200) the failure and the partial repair were unchanged (M0 T5 0.49; M1 − M0 +9.5 [5.6, 13.4] pp), although d still sat at the new ceiling in every split and validation error improved by < 2% (Supplementary Note 1).
6. *Choices of score and unit.* Results are for a perturbation-level score on perturbation-specific genes relative to the training centroid; other scores (per-gene intervals, differential-expression calls) may behave differently.

**Conclusion.** Uncertainty statements for perturbation-response predictions should be audited along effect strength, not only distance. Marginal conformal guarantees are reliable on average but systematically miss the strongest perturbations. Because this heterogeneity of coverage is a heterogeneity of scale, any repair must obtain scale information before the experiment; prior networks provided it for modular, essential-gene programmes, and did not in our test of a non-library target set, leaving experiment-free prediction of perturbation scale as the open problem.
