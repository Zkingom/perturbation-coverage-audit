## Marginally valid split-conformal intervals systematically under-cover the strongest perturbations in three datasets

We calibrated perturbation-level prediction sets for a linear low-rank perturbation model (linear-PCA; score S1, the σ-scaled RMS error on the 50 most perturbation-specific genes) with split conformal prediction at α = 0.1, treating each target gene as the exchangeable unit and holding out whole TSS groups (repeated grouped 7-fold, 3 rounds, 21 splits). Marginal coverage was at its nominal level in all three datasets (K562 essential development 0.90; RPE1 0.896; K562 genome-wide screen with all K562 essential-library targets removed; "non-library target set" for short, 0.903). Coverage, however, collapsed in the top quintile of measured effect strength (tier T5): 0.516 in the development set, 0.480 (95% CI 0.431–0.528; 1,751 genes) in the pre-registered RPE1 confirmation and 0.518 (95% CI 0.490–0.546; 5,683 genes) in the pre-registered confirmation on the non-library target set, with tiers T1–T4 at ≥0.99 throughout (Fig. 1a). In a post hoc illustrative analysis outside the pre-registered protocol, re-tiering the non-library target set perturbations by effect strength corrected for a cell-number-matched noise floor (estimated from non-targeting control cells) gave a T5 coverage of 0.664 (95% CI 0.637–0.690) (Supplementary Fig. S1). The pattern was the same in a second cell line and in a disjoint set of target genes. The same pattern held for a mean-response model and for a GO-embedding linear model (RPE1 T5 under M0: 0.493 and 0.504; the non-library target set at nominal α: 0.520 and 0.517), and on the non-library target set for k = 20 and 200 (0.519, 0.509) and split-half tier assignment (0.507). 

**This pattern is largely fixed by the quantile construction.** If the score is a strictly increasing function of the stratifying variable, split-conformal sets at 1 − α = 0.9 cover every unit below the 90th percentile and none above it, so in effect-strength quintiles T1–T4 are covered with probability 1 and T5 with probability one half (Methods, *Constructive reference*). Monte Carlo simulation with the actual calibration and test sizes gives 0.517, 0.508 and 0.504 (development, RPE1, non-library target set; archived script, Methods). The mean-response model is an exact instance of this case, because its score equals measured effect strength; its T5 coverage was 0.511, 0.493 and 0.520. Observed T5 coverage of linear-PCA and linear-GO differed from the mean-model realisation of this reference by −1.3 to +1.5 percentage points, and from the simulated reference by −2.8 to +1.4 percentage points, in the three datasets (Supplementary Table S8). The largest deviation, linear-PCA on RPE1 (T5 coverage 0.480, simulated reference 0.508), lies well inside the split-to-split spread of the reference (SD of single-split T5 coverage 10.8 percentage points in the simulation), and for the primary model in each dataset the 95% confidence interval of the pooled T5 coverage contains the simulated reference (development 0.47–0.56, RPE1 0.43–0.53, non-library target set 0.49–0.55). The near-complete coverage of T1–T4 is therefore a necessary consequence of the same construction and is not evidence that weak perturbations are well calibrated. What remains informative is how far a predictor's score departs from being a function of effect strength, which we quantify below. Effect-size dependence of prediction difficulty itself is known (Li et al., *Sci. Adv.* 2026, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aed3414); our point concerns how stratified coverage should be read.

## Similarity- and model-uncertainty-based difficulty proxies did not improve top-tier coverage

Under randomised width matching to M0 (same expected mean interval width), none of six deployable distance- or uncertainty-based proxies improved T5 coverage in either confirmation set (Fig. 1b). On RPE1 and the non-library target set, respectively: similarity/training-quality difficulty, normalised +1.7 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 3.6) / +0.3 pp (95% CI -0.4 to 1.0), Mondrian -1.2 pp (95% CI -4.1 to 1.6) / -0.8 pp (95% CI -1.9 to 0.4); Mondrian on raw GO-distance quintiles -1.1 pp (95% CI -3.7 to 1.4) / -1.3 pp (95% CI -2.6 to -0.1); on STRING-distance quintiles +0.1 pp (95% CI -3.5 to 3.7) / -0.7 pp (95% CI -2.1 to 0.7); and a PRESCRIBE-like bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy, normalised -4.6 pp (95% CI -9.7 to 0.6) / -10.5 pp (95% CI -13.4 to -7.7), Mondrian -1.8 pp (95% CI -3.4 to -0.2) / -0.6 pp (95% CI -1.6 to 0.4). The development-set advantage of the Mondrian similarity proxy (+5.7 pp) did not replicate. The bootstrap proxy is one implementation for a linear low-rank model and does not reproduce PRESCRIBE itself. On all three datasets, adjusted for noise, cell number and other covariates, distance terms showed no robust association with miscoverage for linear-PCA; for linear-GO, GO distance retained a modest positive association on the development set and RPE1 but not on the non-library target set (descriptive).

## How much of the score is independent of effect scale

**Heterogeneous coverage is heterogeneous scale (descriptive).** The conformity score is close to proportional to measured effect strength: the ratio S1/E_true has a coefficient of variation of 0.107 (development), 0.097 (RPE1) and 0.008 (non-library target set), with Spearman correlations between S1 and effect strength of 0.94, 0.96 and 1.00; for the mean-response model the two are identical by construction. The fitted models remove only 4.3%, 4.3% and 0.1% of the mean-model error in the three datasets (linear-GO 8.5%, 6.5% and 0.2%), so the score is largely the perturbation's actual scale rather than model-specific residual; on the non-library target set the two are nearly indistinguishable, which makes the scale argument close to definitional there (Supplementary Fig. S2, Table S6, Supplementary Note 1 §5). A reference method that normalises the score by the measured effect strength therefore brings T5 coverage to 0.998 / 0.999 / 0.988 (development / RPE1 / non-library target set) largely because it divides out that scale — for the mean model definitionally so, and on the non-library target set near-definitionally. This reference is not a width-matched comparison: at the lowest α of the matching grid its intervals were still narrower than those of M0 in 20 of 21 RPE1 and 21 of 21 non-library-target-set splits (realised mean width 0.67–1.00 and 0.65–0.75 of M0; Supplementary Table S1), so we do not report a width-matched gain for it. We read it only as a reference for the case in which the actual scale of each perturbation is known; because that scale is what tiers are defined on, its coverage is partly definitional. We summarise the residual by R, the share of the variance of log S1 not explained by log effect strength; R = 0 for the mean model and R = 0.065 for linear-PCA on RPE1 (development 0.112; Supplementary Table S8).

## A non-linear model did not detectably reduce the score–scale coupling (post-confirmation)

To ask whether this coupling is specific to linear models, we trained GEARS on RPE1 with the same 21 splits and scored its predictions with the same S1, references and test units, under a protocol frozen before the run (post-confirmation tier; Methods). GEARS was more accurate than linear-PCA: it removed 6.3% of the mean-model error overall and 9.7% in T5, against 4.3% and 7.9% for linear-PCA (paired differences +1.9 percentage points, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.4, and +1.8, 95% CI 0.8 to 2.8). Its scale-independent share was R = 0.059 (linear-PCA 0.065), a ratio of 0.91 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.11); the coefficient of variation of S1/E_true was 0.112 versus 0.097 (ratio 1.16, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.27). Under the pre-specified rule this is "not reduced": we detected no reduction of the coupling. Because the interval for the R ratio includes 1, this is an absence of evidence for a reduction, not evidence that GEARS cannot reduce it; the interval is compatible with R changing by roughly 24% downwards to 11% upwards. Split-conformal T5 coverage with GEARS scores was 0.498, against the analytic reference of 0.5, with T1–T4 at 1.000 and marginal coverage 0.899.

Three controls, frozen before any GEARS output was read, calibrate this result (Methods). The blank control (mean model) has R = 0 by construction. The positive control uses each perturbation's own half-A cells to predict its half-B profile; it is not deployable, and it represents what a predictor would achieve if it had all perturbation-specific signal and were limited only by the measurement noise of half the cells. On the same half-data basis it reached R = 0.830 (95% CI 0.788 to 0.867), against 0.039 for linear-PCA and 0.036 for GEARS, and was much more accurate than linear-PCA (paired RER difference +28.9 percentage points overall, 95% CI 27.5 to 30.3; +44.9 in T5) (development: R = 0.980 versus 0.104). Its pre-specified verdict is "threshold reachable": in these data the coupling can be broken by a predictor that tracks each perturbation's own response. This shows that the v1.0 threshold is attainable; it does not by itself establish whether, or which, perturbation-specific signal GEARS misses, because the control uses information — the perturbation's own measured cells — that no prediction of an unseen perturbation has. In the negative control, predictions were permuted among test perturbations; all 20 permutations of linear-PCA and 19 of 20 permutations of GEARS were classified "not reduced" (the remaining GEARS permutation "coupling metrics up with accuracy down"), so the rule did not mistake added, perturbation-unrelated error for decoupling.

## A prior-neighbour proxy for effect strength: one pre-registered transfer test

Because the score is close to a multiple of effect strength, a deployable method that changes stratified coverage needs information about each perturbation's scale before the experiment. We built one such proxy, ĥ, from prior-knowledge neighbours (GO and STRING) of each target using measured responses of training perturbations only, and tested it in the pre-registered confirmations.

*Essential-gene screen (RPE1; cross-cell-line).* ĥ predicted effect strength out of sample (Spearman ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.39–0.47; AUROC for T5 0.74). Normalising the conformity score by ĥ (M1) raised width-matched T5 coverage by +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6) (development: +12.0 pp, 95% CI 8.0 to 16.0), and the pre-registered hierarchical criteria were met (step 1 vs M0; step 2 vs the pre-specified strongest development-set baseline, +10.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 14.0); all guards satisfied). Step 2 passed partly because the comparator failed to replicate, and the repair is partial: M1 T5 coverage was 0.577, far below nominal, and T4 coverage decreased from 1.000 to 0.953. In the decision scenario (top 10% by ĥ), coverage rose by +17.4 pp [12.6, 22.3] with a subset width ratio of 1.38; because the subset is selected by the same ĥ that allocates width, this endpoint shows reallocation of a fixed total width toward perturbations ranked high by ĥ. A score fitted to the same features rather than to effect strength (Mscore-N; +11.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 16.0)) performed similarly. On the development set, feature-set comparisons placed most of ĥ's signal in the prior-neighbour features (descriptive; Supplementary Note 1); the similarity of M1 and Mscore-N on RPE1 is consistent with that but does not by itself attribute the gain to any feature group. Results on RPE1 were consistent under grid-based width matching, k = 20 and 200, split-half tier assignment and for linear-GO. 82% of RPE1 targets overlap the development set, so this confirmation tests generalisation across cell lines, not across genes.

*Non-library target set (cross-gene-set).* ĥ barely predicted effect strength (ρ = 0.11, 95% CI 0.08–0.14; AUROC 0.57, 95% CI 0.55–0.59; class P− under the pre-specified rule). Consistently, the pre-registered step 1 was not passed: M1 changed width-matched T5 coverage by +1.0 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 2.1) (pre-specified margin 5 pp), and Mscore-N by +0.7 pp (95% CI -0.5 to 1.8). With an observed SE of 0.57 pp, power to pass at half the development-set effect was 0.96 (post hoc scenario power at the observed SE, using the development-set effect size; the pre-specified C0 power was 0.81 for the same scenario), so the outcome is unlikely to be explained by low power alone; the confidence interval is consistent with any true effect up to about 2 pp for this proxy, this score and this model. In the decision scenario coverage rose by +4.4 pp [3.1, 5.7], with a subset width ratio of 1.09. Other guards were met (nominal marginal coverage 0.903; permuted-ĥ control mean -0.9 pp, 0/20 permutations meeting the step-1 criterion). Grid-based width matching gave the same result (+0.8 pp (95% CI -0.3 to 1.9)), as did linear-GO (width-matched T5 coverage 0.517 under M0 and 0.526 under M1). Pre-specified classification: "step 1 not passed: repair not confirmed".

Subsets that can be named before the experiment also under-covered: the 10% of test perturbations with the highest ĥ were covered 0.740, 0.708 and 0.822 (development, RPE1, non-library target set) under M0. These deficits agree within 3.1 percentage points with what the subsets' share of T5 units alone predicts (Supplementary Table S7), so they follow from the same construction rather than adding independent evidence (post-confirmation, descriptive).

Taken together, the prior-neighbour proxy passed the pre-registered transfer test across cell lines within essential genes (RPE1) and failed it on the non-library target set, where it barely predicted effect strength. We present this as a conditional, secondary result.

## Post-confirmation exploratory analysis (summary)

In analyses specified after unblinding (protocol frozen before running; Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. S_explore), effect strength was highly repeatable across cell halves in all three datasets (Spearman–Brown reliability ≈ 0.99; non-library target set 0.992), and attenuation correction or a cross-split definition of effect strength (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left the proxy's predictability on the non-library target set essentially unchanged (ρ = 0.110 and 0.119), so measurement noise in the outcome did not account for the proxy's poor predictability there. Effect strength was network-autocorrelated among essential genes but hardly among non-essential genes (STRING neighbour correlation 0.41 vs 0.11), and within each dataset the proxy predicted effect strength better for CORUM complex members and highly connected genes. Because ĥ is itself built from neighbours' effect strengths, these observations are partly circular and are offered as a hypothesis (Discussion). M0 coverage of CORUM complex members (0.886, 0.890, 0.892) was fully accounted for by their T5 share (Supplementary Table S9), whereas highly connected genes were covered somewhat better than their T5 share predicts on the two essential-gene screens but not on the non-library target set; we record the latter as a hypothesis generated after unblinding (Supplementary Note 1 §6).


## Confounding

In a logistic model of M0 miscoverage, log effect strength retained a strong positive association after adjustment for split-half noise and reliability, cell number, GO and STRING distance, target baseline expression, GO similarity and shared neighbours (split-half version, RPE1: 4.50 → 3.63 log-odds per SD; development 5.30 → 5.21). On the non-library target set the association was at least as strong (split-half version +7.44 [6.78, 8.23] unadjusted, +5.52 [4.21, 6.77] adjusted; full version quasi-separated, Firth bootstrap, sign only). Target baseline expression was negatively associated with miscoverage in all three datasets (non-library target set, split-half -0.35 [-0.54, -0.19]; mechanism not established). Noise and cell number were collinear and are not interpreted separately; logistic coefficients are non-collapsible, so changes between unadjusted and adjusted models are not read as the share explained by confounding.

## Biological characterisation (development set, descriptive)

Perturbations persistently miscovered across models were essentially the strongest-effect perturbations (78 of 93 core genes were among the 93 highest-effect genes) and were enriched for RNA exosome, nuclear RNA decay, RNA 3′-end processing, RNA polymerase II/TFIID, ribosome and rRNA processing terms. Strong-effect (T5) targets were more often CORUM complex members (78% vs 64%; OR 1.96, p = 6×10⁻⁷) and had more STRING partners; after adjustment for STRING degree and GO annotation count, complex membership was no longer independently associated (+0.10 log-odds per SD, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.24), interpreted cautiously given the overlap between STRING database evidence and CORUM and annotation bias toward well-studied genes. The post-confirmation exploratory analysis above relates this to the predictability of the proxy.
