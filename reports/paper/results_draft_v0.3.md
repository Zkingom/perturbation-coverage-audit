# Results (draft v0.3, 2026-10-15; development set, RPE1 and GWPS, plus post-confirmation exploratory analysis)

*Draft for internal review. Development-set numbers carry selection optimism (methods and thresholds were chosen on the same units). For GWPS the confirmatory analysis was computed first and then recomputed within the full descriptive run; all confirmatory blocks agreed exactly. No confirmatory result has been revised after it was observed.*

## Marginally valid split-conformal intervals systematically under-cover the strongest perturbations in three datasets

We calibrated perturbation-level prediction sets for a linear low-rank perturbation model (linear-PCA; score S1, the σ-scaled RMS error on the 50 most perturbation-specific genes) with split conformal prediction at α = 0.1, treating each target gene as the exchangeable unit and holding out whole TSS groups (repeated grouped 7-fold, 3 rounds, 21 splits). Marginal coverage was at its nominal level in all three datasets (K562 essential development 0.90; RPE1 0.896; K562 genome-wide screen restricted to non-essential targets, GWPS, 0.903). Coverage, however, collapsed in the top quintile of measured effect strength (tier T5): 0.516 in the development set, 0.480 (95% CI 0.431–0.528; 1,751 genes) in the pre-registered RPE1 confirmation and 0.518 (95% CI 0.490–0.546; 5,683 genes) in the pre-registered GWPS confirmation, with tiers T1–T4 at ≥0.99 throughout (Fig. 1a). The failure therefore replicated across a second cell line and across a disjoint set of non-essential target genes. The same pattern held for a mean-response model and for a GO-embedding linear model (RPE1 T5 under M0: 0.493 and 0.504; GWPS at nominal α: 0.520 and 0.517), and on GWPS for k = 20 and 200 (0.519, 0.509) and split-half tier assignment (0.507). Because S1 for the mean model equals the true effect strength, under-coverage of strong perturbations is partly a consequence of how prediction error scales with effect size; we treat effect strength as the explanatory variable for the failure, not as a novel observation (effect-size dependence of prediction difficulty: Li et al., *Sci. Adv.* 2026, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aed3414).

## Similarity- and model-uncertainty-based difficulty proxies do not track this failure axis

Under randomised width matching to M0 (same expected mean interval width), none of six deployable distance- or uncertainty-based proxies improved T5 coverage in either confirmation set (Fig. 1b). On RPE1 / GWPS respectively: similarity/training-quality difficulty, normalised +1.7 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 3.6) / +0.3 pp (95% CI -0.4 to 1.0), Mondrian -1.2 pp (95% CI -4.1 to 1.6) / -0.8 pp (95% CI -1.9 to 0.4); Mondrian on raw GO-distance quintiles -1.1 pp (95% CI -3.7 to 1.4) / -1.3 pp (95% CI -2.6 to -0.1); on STRING-distance quintiles +0.1 pp (95% CI -3.5 to 3.7) / -0.7 pp (95% CI -2.1 to 0.7); and a PRESCRIBE-like bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy, normalised -4.6 pp (95% CI -9.7 to 0.6) / -10.5 pp (95% CI -13.4 to -7.7), Mondrian -1.8 pp (95% CI -3.4 to -0.2) / -0.6 pp (95% CI -1.6 to 0.4). The development-set advantage of the Mondrian similarity proxy (+5.7 pp) did not replicate. The bootstrap proxy is one implementation for a linear low-rank model and does not reproduce PRESCRIBE itself. On RPE1 and the development set, adjusted for noise, cell number and other covariates, distance terms showed no robust association with miscoverage for linear-PCA (for linear-GO, GO distance retained a modest positive association); the corresponding GWPS analysis is pending.

## The bottleneck of repair is predicting effect strength before the experiment

An oracle that normalises the score by the measured effect strength restores T5 coverage almost completely (width-matched T5 gain +52.0 pp (95% CI 47.1 to 56.8) on RPE1 and +47.0 pp (95% CI 44.1 to 49.9) on GWPS; T5 coverage 0.999 and 0.988). A deployable repair therefore depends on how well effect strength can be predicted from information available before the experiment (Fig. 1c). We built such a proxy, ĥ, from prior-knowledge neighbours (GO and STRING) of each target using measured responses of training perturbations only.

*Essential genes (RPE1; cross-cell-line).* ĥ predicted effect strength out of sample (Spearman ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.39–0.47; AUROC for T5 0.74). Normalising the conformity score by ĥ (M1) raised width-matched T5 coverage by +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6) (development: +12.0 pp, 95% CI 8.0 to 16.0), and the pre-registered hierarchical criteria were met (step 1 vs M0; step 2 vs the pre-specified strongest development-set baseline, +10.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 14.0); all guards satisfied). Step 2 passed partly because the comparator failed to replicate, and the repair is partial: M1 T5 coverage was 0.577, far below nominal, and T4 coverage decreased from 1.000 to 0.953. In the decision scenario (top 10% by ĥ), coverage rose by +17.4 pp [12.6, 22.3] with a subset width ratio of 1.38; because the subset is selected by the same ĥ that allocates width, this endpoint shows reallocation of a fixed total width toward perturbations ranked high by ĥ. A score fitted to the same features (Mscore-N; +11.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 16.0)) performed similarly, so the gain derives from the prior-neighbour features. Results on RPE1 were consistent under grid-based width matching, k = 20 and 200, split-half tier assignment and for linear-GO. 82% of RPE1 targets overlap the development set, so this confirmation tests generalisation across cell lines, not across genes.

*Non-essential genes (GWPS; cross-gene-set).* ĥ barely predicted effect strength (ρ = 0.11, 95% CI 0.08–0.14; AUROC 0.57, 95% CI 0.55–0.59; class P− under the pre-specified rule). Consistently, the pre-registered step 1 was not passed: M1 changed width-matched T5 coverage by +1.0 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 2.1) (pre-specified margin 5 pp), and Mscore-N by +0.7 pp (95% CI -0.5 to 1.8). With an observed SE of 0.57 pp, power to pass at half the development-set effect was 0.96; the result therefore bounds any repair in this population to about 2 pp rather than reflecting low power. In the decision scenario coverage rose by +4.4 pp [3.1, 5.7], with a subset width ratio of 1.09. Other guards were met (nominal marginal coverage 0.903; permuted-ĥ control mean -0.9 pp, 0/20 permutations meeting the step-1 criterion). Grid-based width matching gave the same result (+0.8 pp (95% CI -0.3 to 1.9)), as did linear-GO (width-matched T5 coverage 0.517 under M0 and 0.526 under M1). Pre-specified classification: "step 1 not passed: repair not confirmed".

Taken together, the failure axis (effect strength) and its oracle repair generalise across datasets, whereas the prior-neighbour proxy transfers across cell lines within essential genes but not to non-essential genes. We therefore present the repair as a conditional, secondary result whose bottleneck is the experiment-free prediction of effect strength.

## Post-confirmation exploratory analysis: why the proxy fails on non-essential genes

*This analysis was specified after both confirmations were unblinded (protocol frozen before running; reports/protocol/explore_modularity_v1.0.md) and does not alter any confirmatory conclusion.*

*Measurement noise does not explain the failure.* Split-half reliability of effect strength (Spearman–Brown, cells split into halves A and B) was ≈0.99 in all three datasets (GWPS 0.992, 95% CI 0.992–0.993), and correcting ρ(ĥ, E) for attenuation left it essentially unchanged (GWPS 0.110). By the pre-specified rule, low predictability on GWPS is attributed to a lack of information in the prior neighbours rather than to noise in E. Split-half reliability measures repeatability across cell halves, so components shared by both halves (for example a cell-number-dependent noise floor) count as signal; it is not a measure of validity. The GWPS distribution of log effect strength was also narrower (SD 0.34 vs 0.44 on RPE1), which may contribute to lower ρ. Removing noise shared between width allocation and coverage assessment (allocating width with half A, assessing coverage with half B) lowered oracle T5 coverage only slightly (nominal α; GWPS 0.898 → 0.849; RPE1 0.990 → 0.994), so the attainable repair remains large.

*Effect strength is network-modular among essential but not non-essential genes.* The correlation between a gene's effect strength and the STRING-weighted mean of its neighbours' effect strengths was 0.41 on RPE1 and 0.11 on GWPS (difference 0.29, 95% CI 0.23–0.35). Within every dataset, ĥ predicted effect strength better for CORUM complex members and for high-STRING-degree targets (high minus low degree tertile: RPE1 Δρ = +0.40, GWPS +0.14; 7 of 8 pre-specified contrasts significant after Holm correction; Fig. 1d). Most GWPS targets lie in sparse network regions (66% below the RPE1 lower degree tertile), but a gap remained within matched degree strata (RPE1 minus GWPS Δρ 0.11–0.30), so network composition explains only part of the difference. Descriptively, the repair concentrated in densely connected genes (GWPS high-degree, fixed cut-points: +10.2 pp) and width reallocation reduced T5 coverage for the lowest-degree tertile in all three datasets (e.g. RPE1 -14.9 pp). Two caveats apply. Because ĥ is built from neighbours' effect strengths, network autocorrelation and ĥ predictability overlap conceptually. And STRING degree partly reflects how well a gene has been studied.

## Confounding

In a logistic model of M0 miscoverage, log effect strength retained a strong positive association after adjustment for split-half noise and reliability, cell number, GO and STRING distance, target baseline expression, GO similarity and shared neighbours (split-half version, RPE1: 4.50 → 3.63 log-odds per SD; development 5.30 → 5.21). On GWPS the association was at least as strong (split-half version +7.44 [6.78, 8.23] unadjusted, +5.52 [4.21, 6.77] adjusted; full version quasi-separated, Firth bootstrap, sign only). Target baseline expression was negatively associated with miscoverage in all three datasets (GWPS split-half -0.35 [-0.54, -0.19]; mechanism not established). Noise and cell number were collinear and are not interpreted separately; logistic coefficients are non-collapsible, so changes between unadjusted and adjusted models are not read as the share explained by confounding.

## Biological characterisation (development set, descriptive)

Perturbations persistently miscovered across models were essentially the strongest-effect perturbations (78 of 93 core genes were among the 93 highest-effect genes) and were enriched for RNA exosome, nuclear RNA decay, RNA 3′-end processing, RNA polymerase II/TFIID, ribosome and rRNA processing terms. Strong-effect (T5) targets were more often CORUM complex members (78% vs 64%; OR 1.96, p = 6×10⁻⁷) and had more STRING partners; after adjustment for STRING degree and GO annotation count, complex membership was no longer independently associated (+0.10 log-odds per SD, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.24), interpreted cautiously given the overlap between STRING database evidence and CORUM and annotation bias toward well-studied genes. The post-confirmation exploratory analysis above relates this to the predictability of the proxy.

## Limitations

- All datasets come from one laboratory and platform (Replogle et al. 2022); there is no cross-laboratory confirmation.
- RPE1 targets largely overlap the development set (cross-cell-line test); GWPS tests a disjoint gene set but the same cell line as the development set.
- For GWPS, the confirmatory analysis was computed before the descriptive run because of local memory limits (recorded deviation of execution order); the full run recomputed it with identical results. The descriptive run used a memory-saving implementation validated byte-identical against the frozen implementation on the development set.
- Tier assignment uses split-specific references; 2.7% (development) and 3.0% (RPE1) of genes change tier between rounds.
- K and d reached the upper grid bound in all 21 RPE1 splits, but only in 11 of 21 GWPS splits each; a post-confirmation exploratory analysis with an extended grid is planned for RPE1.
