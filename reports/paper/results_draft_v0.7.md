## Marginally valid split-conformal intervals systematically under-cover the strongest perturbations in three datasets

We calibrated perturbation-level prediction sets for a linear low-rank perturbation model (linear-PCA; score S1, the σ-scaled RMS error on the 50 most perturbation-specific genes) with split conformal prediction at α = 0.1, treating each target gene as the exchangeable unit and holding out whole TSS groups (repeated grouped 7-fold, 3 rounds, 21 splits). Marginal coverage was at its nominal level in all three datasets (K562 essential development 0.90; RPE1 0.896; K562 genome-wide screen with all K562 essential-library targets removed; "non-library target set" for short, 0.903). Coverage, however, collapsed in the top quintile of measured effect strength (tier T5): 0.516 in the development set, 0.480 (95% CI 0.431–0.528; 1,751 genes) in the pre-registered RPE1 confirmation and 0.518 (95% CI 0.490–0.546; 5,683 genes) in the pre-registered confirmation on the non-library target set, with tiers T1–T4 at ≥0.99 throughout (Fig. 1a). In a post hoc illustrative analysis outside the pre-registered protocol, re-tiering the non-library target set perturbations by effect strength corrected for a cell-number-matched noise floor (estimated from non-targeting control cells) gave a T5 coverage of 0.664 (95% CI 0.637–0.690) (Supplementary Fig. S_noise). The failure therefore replicated across a second cell line and across a disjoint set of non-essential target genes. The same pattern held for a mean-response model and for a GO-embedding linear model (RPE1 T5 under M0: 0.493 and 0.504; the non-library target set at nominal α: 0.520 and 0.517), and on the non-library target set for k = 20 and 200 (0.519, 0.509) and split-half tier assignment (0.507). Because S1 for the mean model equals the true effect strength, under-coverage of strong perturbations is partly a consequence of how prediction error scales with effect size; we treat effect strength as the explanatory variable for the failure, not as a novel observation (effect-size dependence of prediction difficulty: Li et al., *Sci. Adv.* 2026, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aed3414).

## Similarity- and model-uncertainty-based difficulty proxies do not track this failure axis

Under randomised width matching to M0 (same expected mean interval width), none of six deployable distance- or uncertainty-based proxies improved T5 coverage in either confirmation set (Fig. 1b). On RPE1 and the non-library target set, respectively: similarity/training-quality difficulty, normalised +1.7 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 3.6) / +0.3 pp (95% CI -0.4 to 1.0), Mondrian -1.2 pp (95% CI -4.1 to 1.6) / -0.8 pp (95% CI -1.9 to 0.4); Mondrian on raw GO-distance quintiles -1.1 pp (95% CI -3.7 to 1.4) / -1.3 pp (95% CI -2.6 to -0.1); on STRING-distance quintiles +0.1 pp (95% CI -3.5 to 3.7) / -0.7 pp (95% CI -2.1 to 0.7); and a PRESCRIBE-like bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy, normalised -4.6 pp (95% CI -9.7 to 0.6) / -10.5 pp (95% CI -13.4 to -7.7), Mondrian -1.8 pp (95% CI -3.4 to -0.2) / -0.6 pp (95% CI -1.6 to 0.4). The development-set advantage of the Mondrian similarity proxy (+5.7 pp) did not replicate. The bootstrap proxy is one implementation for a linear low-rank model and does not reproduce PRESCRIBE itself. On all three datasets, adjusted for noise, cell number and other covariates, distance terms showed no robust association with miscoverage for linear-PCA; for linear-GO, GO distance retained a modest positive association on the development set and RPE1 but not on the non-library target set (descriptive).

## The bottleneck of repair is predicting effect strength before the experiment

**Heterogeneous coverage is heterogeneous scale (descriptive).** The conformity score is close to proportional to measured effect strength: the ratio S1/E_true has a coefficient of variation of 0.107 (development), 0.097 (RPE1) and 0.008 (non-library target set), with Spearman correlations between S1 and effect strength of 0.94, 0.96 and 1.00; for the mean-response model the two are identical by construction. A reference method that normalises the score by the measured effect strength therefore brings T5 coverage to 0.998 / 0.999 / 0.988 (development / RPE1 / non-library target set) largely because it divides out that scale — for the mean model definitionally so, and on the non-library target set near-definitionally. This reference is not a width-matched comparison: at the lowest α of the matching grid its intervals were still narrower than those of M0 in 20 of 21 RPE1 and 21 of 21 non-library-target-set splits (realised mean width 0.67–1.00 and 0.65–0.75 of M0; Supplementary Table S_oracle), so we do not report a width-matched gain for it. We read it only as what is attainable when the actual scale of each perturbation is known. The consequence for a deployable method is a statement about scale, not about biology: any repair must obtain scale information before the experiment, and how well prior knowledge predicts that scale is an open question. We built one such proxy, ĥ, from prior-knowledge neighbours (GO and STRING) of each target using measured responses of training perturbations only.

*Essential-gene screen (RPE1; cross-cell-line).* ĥ predicted effect strength out of sample (Spearman ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.39–0.47; AUROC for T5 0.74). Normalising the conformity score by ĥ (M1) raised width-matched T5 coverage by +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6) (development: +12.0 pp, 95% CI 8.0 to 16.0), and the pre-registered hierarchical criteria were met (step 1 vs M0; step 2 vs the pre-specified strongest development-set baseline, +10.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 14.0); all guards satisfied). Step 2 passed partly because the comparator failed to replicate, and the repair is partial: M1 T5 coverage was 0.577, far below nominal, and T4 coverage decreased from 1.000 to 0.953. In the decision scenario (top 10% by ĥ), coverage rose by +17.4 pp [12.6, 22.3] with a subset width ratio of 1.38; because the subset is selected by the same ĥ that allocates width, this endpoint shows reallocation of a fixed total width toward perturbations ranked high by ĥ. A score fitted to the same features rather than to effect strength (Mscore-N; +11.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 16.0)) performed similarly. On the development set, feature-set comparisons placed most of ĥ's signal in the prior-neighbour features (descriptive; Supplementary Note 1); the similarity of M1 and Mscore-N on RPE1 is consistent with that but does not by itself attribute the gain to any feature group. Results on RPE1 were consistent under grid-based width matching, k = 20 and 200, split-half tier assignment and for linear-GO. 82% of RPE1 targets overlap the development set, so this confirmation tests generalisation across cell lines, not across genes.

*Non-library target set (cross-gene-set).* ĥ barely predicted effect strength (ρ = 0.11, 95% CI 0.08–0.14; AUROC 0.57, 95% CI 0.55–0.59; class P− under the pre-specified rule). Consistently, the pre-registered step 1 was not passed: M1 changed width-matched T5 coverage by +1.0 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 2.1) (pre-specified margin 5 pp), and Mscore-N by +0.7 pp (95% CI -0.5 to 1.8). With an observed SE of 0.57 pp, power to pass at half the development-set effect was 0.96 (post hoc scenario power at the observed SE, using the development-set effect size; the pre-specified C0 power was 0.81 for the same scenario), so the outcome is unlikely to be explained by low power alone; the confidence interval is consistent with any true effect up to about 2 pp for this proxy, this score and this model. In the decision scenario coverage rose by +4.4 pp [3.1, 5.7], with a subset width ratio of 1.09. Other guards were met (nominal marginal coverage 0.903; permuted-ĥ control mean -0.9 pp, 0/20 permutations meeting the step-1 criterion). Grid-based width matching gave the same result (+0.8 pp (95% CI -0.3 to 1.9)), as did linear-GO (width-matched T5 coverage 0.517 under M0 and 0.526 under M1). Pre-specified classification: "step 1 not passed: repair not confirmed".

Taken together, the failure along effect strength replicated in both confirmation sets, whereas the prior-neighbour proxy transferred across cell lines within essential genes but not to the non-essential target set. We therefore present the repair as a conditional, secondary result: because coverage heterogeneity is scale heterogeneity, a deployable repair needs experiment-free information about each perturbation's scale, and prior-network neighbours supply that information for modular essential-gene programmes but not, in this test, for non-essential targets.

## Post-confirmation exploratory analysis (summary)

In analyses specified after unblinding (protocol frozen before running; Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. S_explore), effect strength was highly repeatable across cell halves in all three datasets (Spearman–Brown reliability ≈ 0.99; non-library target set 0.992), and attenuation correction or a cross-split definition of effect strength (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left the proxy's predictability on the non-library target set essentially unchanged (ρ = 0.110 and 0.119), arguing against measurement noise in the outcome as the reason for the failure on non-essential genes. Effect strength was network-autocorrelated among essential genes but hardly among non-essential genes (STRING neighbour correlation 0.41 vs 0.11), and within each dataset the proxy predicted effect strength better for CORUM complex members and highly connected genes. Because ĥ is itself built from neighbours' effect strengths, these observations are partly circular and are offered as a hypothesis (Discussion).


## Confounding

In a logistic model of M0 miscoverage, log effect strength retained a strong positive association after adjustment for split-half noise and reliability, cell number, GO and STRING distance, target baseline expression, GO similarity and shared neighbours (split-half version, RPE1: 4.50 → 3.63 log-odds per SD; development 5.30 → 5.21). On the non-library target set the association was at least as strong (split-half version +7.44 [6.78, 8.23] unadjusted, +5.52 [4.21, 6.77] adjusted; full version quasi-separated, Firth bootstrap, sign only). Target baseline expression was negatively associated with miscoverage in all three datasets (non-library target set, split-half -0.35 [-0.54, -0.19]; mechanism not established). Noise and cell number were collinear and are not interpreted separately; logistic coefficients are non-collapsible, so changes between unadjusted and adjusted models are not read as the share explained by confounding.

## Biological characterisation (development set, descriptive)

Perturbations persistently miscovered across models were essentially the strongest-effect perturbations (78 of 93 core genes were among the 93 highest-effect genes) and were enriched for RNA exosome, nuclear RNA decay, RNA 3′-end processing, RNA polymerase II/TFIID, ribosome and rRNA processing terms. Strong-effect (T5) targets were more often CORUM complex members (78% vs 64%; OR 1.96, p = 6×10⁻⁷) and had more STRING partners; after adjustment for STRING degree and GO annotation count, complex membership was no longer independently associated (+0.10 log-odds per SD, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.24), interpreted cautiously given the overlap between STRING database evidence and CORUM and annotation bias toward well-studied genes. The post-confirmation exploratory analysis above relates this to the predictability of the proxy.

## Limitations

- All datasets come from one laboratory and platform (Replogle et al. 2022); there is no cross-laboratory confirmation.
- RPE1 targets largely overlap the development set (cross-cell-line test); the non-library target set tests a disjoint gene set but the same cell line as the development set.
- For the non-library target set, the confirmatory analysis was computed before the descriptive run because of local memory limits (recorded deviation of execution order); the full run recomputed it with identical results. The descriptive run used a memory-saving implementation validated byte-identical against the frozen implementation on the development set.
- Tier assignment uses split-specific references; 2.7% (development) and 3.0% (RPE1) of genes change tier between rounds.
- K and d reached the upper grid bound in all 21 RPE1 splits, but only in 11 of 21 non-library-target-set splits each. A post-confirmation exploratory analysis on RPE1 with extended grids (K up to 200, d up to 200; protocol frozen before running) left the failure and the partial repair unchanged (M0 T5 coverage 0.491; M1 − M0 +9.5 pp, 95% CI 5.6 to 13.4), with d again at the new ceiling in all 21 splits and validation error improved by < 2% (Supplementary Note 1 §4).
- Evaluation units require at least 30 cells. The perturbations excluded by this rule have systematically higher measured effect strength than the included ones (median 7.9 vs 3.6, 6.6 vs 3.9 and 9.6 vs 3.2 for development, RPE1 and the non-library target set, respectively; 73–100% of them above the T5 cut-point of the included units), so the audit is conducted after truncating that tail. Because measured effect strength also rises as cell number falls, the direction of this selection effect on the coverage estimate cannot be signed (descriptive; Methods).
