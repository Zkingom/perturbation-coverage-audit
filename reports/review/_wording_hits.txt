results:7: We calibrated perturbation-level prediction sets for a linear low-rank perturbation model (linear-PCA; score S1, the σ-scaled RMS error on the 50 most perturbation-specific genes) with split conformal prediction at α = 0.1, treating each target gene as the exchangeable unit and holding out whole TSS groups (repeated grouped 7-fold, 3 rounds, 21 splits). Marginal coverage was at its nominal level in all three datasets (K562 essential development 0.90; RPE1 0.896; K562 genome-wide screen restricted to non-essential targets, GWPS, 0.903). Coverage, however, collapsed in the top quintile of measured effect strength (tier T5): 0.516 in the development set, 0.480 (95% CI 0.431–0.528; 1,751 genes) in the pre-registered RPE1 confirmation and 0.518 (95% CI 0.490–0.546; 5,683 genes) in the pre-registered GWPS confirmation, with tiers T1–T4 at ≥0.99 throughout (Fig. 1a). In a post hoc illustrative analysis outside the pre-registered protocol, re-tiering GWPS perturbations by effect strength corrected for a cell-number-matched noise floor (estimated from non-targeting control cells) gave a T5 coverage of 0.664 (95% CI 0.637–0.690) (Supplementary Fig. S_noise). The failure therefore replicated across a second cell line and across a disjoint set of non-essential target genes. The same pattern held for a mean-response model and for a GO-embedding linear model (RPE1 T5 under M0: 0.493 and 0.504; GWPS at nominal α: 0.520 and 0.517), and on GWPS for k = 20 and 200 (0.519, 0.509) and split-half tier assignment (0.507). Because S1 for the mean model equals the true effect strength, under-coverage of strong perturbations is partly a consequence of how prediction error scales with effect size; we treat effect strength as the explanatory variable for the failure, not as a novel observation (effect-size dependence of prediction difficulty: Li et al., *Sci. Adv.* 2026, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aed3414).
results:11: Under randomised width matching to M0 (same expected mean interval width), none of six deployable distance- or uncertainty-based proxies improved T5 coverage in either confirmation set (Fig. 1b). The bootstrap proxy is one implementation for a linear low-rank model and does not reproduce PRESCRIBE itself. On RPE1 and the development set, adjusted for noise, cell number and other covariates, distance terms showed no robust association with miscoverage for linear-PCA (for linear-GO, GO distance retained a modest positive association); the corresponding GWPS analysis is pending.
results:15: An oracle that normalises the score by the measured effect strength restores T5 coverage almost completely (width-matched T5 gain +52.0 pp (95% CI 47.1 to 56.8) on RPE1 and +47.0 pp (95% CI 44.1 to 49.9) on GWPS; T5 coverage 0.999 and 0.988). A deployable repair therefore depends on how well effect strength can be predicted from information available before the experiment (Fig. 1c). We built such a proxy, ĥ, from prior-knowledge neighbours (GO and STRING) of each target using measured responses of training perturbations only.
results:17: *Essential genes (RPE1; cross-cell-line).* ĥ predicted effect strength out of sample (Spearman ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.39–0.47; AUROC for T5 0.74). Normalising the conformity score by ĥ (M1) raised width-matched T5 coverage by +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6) (development: +12.0 pp, 95% CI 8.0 to 16.0), and the pre-registered hierarchical criteria were met (step 1 vs M0; step 2 vs the pre-specified strongest development-set baseline, +10.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 14.0); all guards satisfied). Step 2 passed partly because the comparator failed to replicate, and the repair is partial: M1 T5 coverage was 0.577, far below nominal, and T4 coverage decreased from 1.000 to 0.953. In the decision scenario (top 10% by ĥ), coverage rose by +17.4 pp [12.6, 22.3] with a subset width ratio of 1.38; because the subset is selected by the same ĥ that allocates width, this endpoint shows reallocation of a fixed total width toward perturbations ranked high by ĥ. A score fitted to the same features (Mscore-N; +11.9 pp (95% CI 7.8 to 16.0)) performed similarly, so the gain derives from the prior-neighbour features. Results on RPE1 were consistent under grid-based width matching, k = 20 and 200, split-half tier assignment and for linear-GO. 82% of RPE1 targets overlap the development set, so this confirmation tests generalisation across cell lines, not across genes.
results:19: *Non-essential genes (GWPS; cross-gene-set).* ĥ barely predicted effect strength (ρ = 0.11, 95% CI 0.08–0.14; AUROC 0.57, 95% CI 0.55–0.59; class P− under the pre-specified rule). results:21: Taken together, the failure axis (effect strength) and its oracle repair generalise across datasets, whereas the prior-neighbour proxy transfers across cell lines within essential genes but not to non-essential genes. We therefore present the repair as a conditional, secondary result whose bottleneck is the experiment-free prediction of effect strength.
results:25: In analyses specified after unblinding (protocol frozen before running; Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. S_explore), effect strength was highly repeatable across cell halves in all three datasets (Spearman–Brown reliability ≈ 0.99; GWPS 0.992), and attenuation correction or a cross-split definition of effect strength (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left the proxy's predictability on GWPS essentially unchanged (ρ = 0.110 and 0.119), arguing against measurement noise in the outcome as the reason for the failure on non-essential genes. methods:7: We used the genome-scale CRISPRi Perturb-seq screens of Replogle et al.¹ (raw single-cell and bulk files, Figshare+ doi:10.25452/figshare.plus.20029387.v1): K562 essential-gene screen (development set), RPE1 essential-gene screen (primary confirmation set) and the K562 genome-wide screen (GWPS; secondary confirmation set) from which all targets of the K562 essential screen were removed, so that GWPS tests generalisation to non-essential genes. File sizes and checksums were verified against the publisher's records (computed MD5 where provided, uploader-supplied MD5 otherwise) and SHA-256 values were recorded. For each perturbation we formed a pseudobulk profile as the mean over cells of log1p(counts per 10,000) and, for split-half analyses, two pseudobulks from a random partition of its cells into halves A and B (fixed seed). Single-cell pseudobulks were checked against the publisher's bulk files (per-perturbation mean UMI; GWPS maximum absolute difference 2.4 × 10⁻⁵, zero elements outside tolerance).
methods:9: **Units and leakage control.** The exchangeable unit is the target gene. Evaluation units ("E units") were targets with at least 30 cells that met the pre-specified inclusion rules; targets sharing a transcription start site within 1 kb (Ensembl GRCh38 coordinates) were merged into TSS groups, because CRISPRi guides can repress neighbouring promoters. All splits assign whole TSS groups; non-E perturbations were always placed in training ("always-train") unless they shared a TSS group with an E unit, in which case they were dropped. Sizes: development 1,786 E units (1,721 TSS groups); RPE1 1,751 genes evaluated; GWPS 5,683 E units (5,449 TSS groups) plus 2,001 always-train units.

discussion:3: **Marginal validity hides a systematic blind spot.** Across three Perturb-seq screens, two cell lines and both essential and non-essential genes, split-conformal prediction sets for a strong linear perturbation model were marginally valid yet covered only about half of the 20% strongest perturbations, while covering weaker perturbations almost always. This is not a small-sample artefact: the pattern holds for 1,751–5,683 target genes, for three response models and for k = 20–200 readout genes, and it survives a split-half test in which effect-strength tiers are defined on one half of the cells and coverage judged on the other. For the biological use of uncertainty estimates — prioritising which predicted perturbations to test, or trusting a predicted strong phenotype — this is the regime that matters most, and it is exactly where a marginal guarantee is least informative. Part of the failure is built into the score: for the mean model the score equals effect strength, so a single global quantile must under-cover the largest effects. The point of the study is that the same failure persists for a fitted model and is not repaired by the usual notion of "distribution shift".
discussion:5: **Distance is the wrong axis.** Out-of-distribution work on perturbation prediction emphasises how far a test perturbation is from the training data. Six deployable proxies built on that idea — GO and STRING distances, similarity- and data-quality-based difficulty, and a bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy in the spirit of PRESCRIBE — produced no T5 gain on either confirmation set, and in confounding analyses distance had no residual association with miscoverage for the primary model once effect strength was accounted for. Effect strength, by contrast, remained the dominant predictor in every dataset. We therefore suggest that calibration audits of perturbation models report coverage stratified by effect strength in addition to similarity-based OOD splits; consistent with this, a recent benchmark found that model rankings depend strongly on perturbation effect size and recommended effect-size-stratified evaluation¹¹, although its direction of dependence varied with the metric and it did not address uncertainty quantification.
discussion:7: **The bottleneck is predicting effect strength before the experiment.** An oracle that knows effect strength restores T5 coverage (≈ +47–52 pp under width matching), and removing measurement noise shared between width allocation and assessment reduced this only modestly. The deployable prior-neighbour proxy recovered a pre-registered, statistically supported part of the gap on RPE1 (E1 +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6)), but not on non-essential GWPS genes (E1 +1.0 pp (95% CI -0.1 to 2.1); well powered). The proxy's predictability explains the difference (ρ = 0.43 vs 0.11), and post-confirmation analyses point to network structure: effect strength was spatially autocorrelated on STRING among essential genes but hardly among non-essential ones, and within every dataset the proxy worked better for complex members and highly connected genes. A plausible biological reading is that essential genes act through dense, co-regulated machines (ribosome, RNA processing, transcription initiation), so a gene's effect is predictable from its partners, whereas non-essential genes act more idiosyncratically. We regard this as a hypothesis, not a finding.
discussion:9: **The repair has a cost.** Normalising by ĥ at equal total width moves width from perturbations predicted to be weak to those predicted to be strong. Perturbations with little prior information pay for this: in the lowest STRING-degree tertile, the change in T5 coverage under M1 was negative in all three datasets, with CIs excluding zero on RPE1 and GWPS (RPE1 -14.9 pp (95% CI -22.4 to -7.5); GWPS -3.7 pp (95% CI -5.5 to -1.8); development -5.1 pp (95% CI -15.8 to 5.5)), whereas the gains concentrated in densely connected genes (GWPS, high-degree stratum at fixed cut-points, +10.2 pp (95% CI 6.0 to 14.3)). These group-wise numbers are descriptive and were not corrected for the nine groups examined. A practitioner should not apply the repair uniformly. One natural extension — enabling the repair only where the proxy is expected to be predictive, for example by network degree — is left to future work; we did not construct or test such a rule on the present data, because any rule tuned here would need new data for a valid confirmation.
discussion:12: 1. *Circularity.* ĥ is built from neighbours' effect strengths, so "low network autocorrelation" and "low ĥ predictability" overlap conceptually and the statement that one explains the other is partly tautological. The information that is not circular is threefold: the split-half reliability of effect strength (≈ 0.99 in all datasets) rules out measurement noise as the reason for low predictability on GWPS; the stratifying effect of network position (degree, complex membership) on predictability and repair is consistent across three datasets; and a cross-dataset gap persists within matched degree strata.
discussion:13: 2. *Reliability is not validity.* Split-half reliability measures repeatability across cell halves; components shared by both halves (for example a cell-number-dependent noise floor in the top-gene selection, or batch) count as signal. An illustrative cross-split analysis (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left ĥ's predictability unchanged (GWPS ρ = 0.119) and reliability stayed high after partialling out cell number (GWPS 0.960). However, on GWPS measured effect strength is strongly anti-correlated with cell number (ρ = -0.80), so part of the strong-effect tier there may reflect measurement precision (a noise floor) rather than biological effect size; this cannot be separated from fitness effects on cell yield with the present data (Supplementary Note 1) On both essential-gene screens, re-tiering perturbations by effect strength corrected for a cell-number-matched null from non-targeting control cells left the failure essentially unchanged (M0 T5 coverage 0.55 and 0.49); on GWPS the same correction raised T5 coverage from 0.52 to 0.66 and 21% of perturbations did not exceed the null, so on non-essential genes a sizeable part of the apparent failure reflects the precision of low-cell-number measurements. Reporting coverage by noise-corrected effect strength should accompany any such audit.
discussion:15: 4. *Scope of the confirmation.* All three screens come from one laboratory and platform, and two are in K562; most RPE1 targets overlap the development targets, so RPE1 tests generalisation across cell lines for largely the same genes, while GWPS tests generalisation to new genes. Cross-laboratory replication (e.g. in recently published perturbation atlases¹²) is needed.
discussion:19: **Conclusion.** Uncertainty statements for perturbation-response predictions should be audited along effect strength, not only distance. Marginal conformal guarantees are reliable on average but systematically miss the strongest perturbations; the fix requires predicting effect strength before the experiment, which prior networks enable for modular, essential-gene programmes but not yet for non-essential genes.

note1:29: *An observation that must be reported.* On GWPS, measured effect strength is strongly anti-correlated with the number of cells per perturbation (ρ = -0.80; development -0.26, RPE1 -0.51). The cross-split definition removes selection inflation and weakens, but does not remove, this coupling (GWPS -0.54; development ≈ 0). Two explanations cannot be separated with these data: (i) a noise floor — with fewer cells the RMS of residual sampling noise on any gene set is larger, which the cross-split construction does not remove; (ii) biology — perturbations with larger transcriptional effects also impair proliferation and therefore yield fewer cells. Under (i), part of the "strong-effect" tier on GWPS reflects measurement precision rather than biological effect size; the coverage failure would still be real for those perturbations, but its interpretation as an effect-strength phenomenon would be weaker on GWPS than on the essential-gene screens. Because cell number is an experimental outcome, it is excluded from ĥ by design, which may contribute to low predictability on GWPS. A noise-floor-corrected effect strength (e.g. subtracting the effect strength of cell-number-matched pseudo-perturbations sampled from non-targeting control cells) would address this; it has not been computed.
note1:34: *Construction.* For each dataset, non-targeting control cells (development 10,691; RPE1 11,485; GWPS: random 20,000 of 75,328) were transformed exactly as in the pseudobulk pipeline. Pseudo-perturbations of n cells were drawn without replacement (geometric grid of 20 values of n from 30 to the largest cell number among E units; 200 draws per n). Their effect strength was computed with the E_true definition and each split's own training reference; control cells are not part of that reference. For each perturbation, E_corr = E_true − m(n) and E_z = (E_true − m(n))/s(n), where m and s are the null mean and SD at its cell number (linear interpolation in log n). Perturbations were re-tiered by E_corr (and E_z) with the same quintile rule, and M0 coverage by the new tiers was estimated with the same GEE as the confirmatory B0. Recomputed E_true and original tiers matched the stored caches exactly, and the original tiers reproduced the confirmatory B0 estimate exactly. Pre-written reading: GWPS M0 T5 coverage under corrected tiers > 0.75 → the GWPS failure is mainly attributable to measurement precision.
