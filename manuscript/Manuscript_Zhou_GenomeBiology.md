# Abstract

**Background:** Models of single-cell perturbation responses are increasingly used to prioritise perturbations for testing, and their uncertainty is often evaluated by stratifying coverage on measured effect size. When the conformity score is close to a multiple of effect strength, such stratified coverage is largely fixed by the quantile construction rather than by the model.

**Results:** We audited split-conformal prediction sets on three CRISPRi Perturb-seq screens under pre-registered protocols, holding out whole transcription-start-site groups. Marginal coverage was nominal, but top-quintile coverage was about one half (RPE1 0.480, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.53; target genes outside the essential library 0.518, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.55), as the constructive reference predicts. We propose the share R of the score not explained by effect strength as a diagnostic of when stratified coverage is informative. R was at most 0.193 on the three screens and stayed small in HepG2 and Jurkat screens analysed under protocols frozen before their data were read (0.086 and 0.038); the non-linear model GEARS, although more accurate than a linear baseline, did not detectably increase it. On combinatorial CRISPRa perturbations, R was 0.662 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.78) for an additive baseline and 0.486 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.60) for a linear model, and point estimates of top-quintile coverage departed from the reference, although gene-resampled intervals included it.

**Conclusions:** Effect-stratified coverage should be reported together with R and its constructive reference. R indicates when stratified coverage can reflect model differences, as on combinatorial perturbations whose single-gene components have been measured.

**Keywords:** conformal prediction; uncertainty quantification; perturbation response prediction; Perturb-seq; CRISPR screens; calibration; pre-registration; combinatorial perturbations

# Background


Pooled CRISPR screens read out by single-cell RNA sequencing now measure the transcriptional consequences of thousands of genetic perturbations in one experiment [1]. A growing family of models predicts the response of perturbations that were not measured [2–4]. The practical promise of such models is triage: deciding which of many candidate perturbations to run next. That use requires more than an accurate point prediction. It requires knowing which predictions can be trusted.

The field's assessment of predictive performance has become considerably more careful. Linear baselines match or exceed deep models on standard benchmarks when the evaluation is set up carefully [2]. The choice of reference point determines whether an apparent effect is perturbation-specific or a systematic offset shared by all perturbations [4]. A recent systematic comparison showed that model rankings depend strongly on the size of a perturbation's effect, and it recommended effect-size-stratified evaluation [5]. These advances concern the accuracy of the prediction. They do not say how an uncertainty statement attached to that prediction should be validated.

Conformal prediction offers a distribution-free way to attach prediction sets with a finite-sample coverage guarantee[6]. The guarantee, however, is marginal: it holds on average over the exchangeable units, and says nothing about any identifiable subgroup. In a screen, the subgroups that matter are exactly the ones a biologist would act on — the perturbations with the largest effects, or those least similar to anything measured before. Methods that restore coverage conditional on a covariate exist[7], and work on perturbation prediction under distribution shift has concentrated on similarity to the training data, including an uncertainty-aware method that calibrates against the difficulty of each perturbation[8]. What has not been established is how a conditional-coverage audit of these screens should be read: whether marginal conformal calibration fails for identifiable perturbations, how much of any such failure is fixed by the way subgroups are defined rather than by the model, and whether a deployable method changes it — in a setting where the answer is tested once on held-out data rather than selected on the same data that produced it.

Here we show that, for the single-gene predictors and screens studied, effect-stratified coverage is largely fixed at one half in the strongest quintile by the quantile construction itself. We introduce a diagnostic framework to separate genuine model calibration from this baseline artefact. In five single-gene screens from four cell lines, the scale-independent share R of the conformity score was small. This meant that stratified coverage reflected the score's scaling with effect size rather than model quality. Even the non-linear model GEARS[3] improved accuracy over the linear baseline without detectably raising R. On combinatorial perturbations[9] whose single-gene components had been measured, the picture differed. R was large for both an additive baseline and a linear model, marking the regime where effect-stratified coverage carries model-specific information. When neither component had been measured, though, the linear model showed no measurable accuracy gain over the mean-response model. A deployable prior-neighbour proxy for effect scale met its pre-registered repair criteria across cell lines within essential genes, but not on a disjoint gene set. To obtain these results, we carried out three steps. (i) We derive the constructive reference for effect-stratified coverage when the conformity score scales with effect strength, and define R as the share of log-score variance not explained by log effect strength. (ii) We audit stratified coverage and R across three CRISPRi Perturb-seq screens under a pre-registered protocol, with target genes as exchangeable units and whole transcription-start-site (TSS) groups held out to prevent promoter-repression leakage. All analysis choices were frozen with cryptographic hashes on a development screen before any confirmation response entered a score. (iii) Under protocols frozen before the data were read, we test the predictions that R remains small in two further single-gene screens and rises on combinatorial perturbations.

# Results


## Marginally valid split-conformal intervals cover only half of the strongest perturbations, a pattern largely fixed by construction

We found that marginally valid split-conformal prediction sets cover only about half of the top-quintile perturbations across three CRISPRi screens. This pattern is largely fixed by the quantile construction when the conformity score is close to a multiple of effect strength. So effect-stratified coverage says little about the model unless it is read against its constructive reference. We calibrated perturbation-level prediction sets for linear-PCA (score S1, the σ-scaled RMS error on the 50 most perturbation-specific genes) using split-conformal prediction at α = 0.1. We used 21 grouped splits (3 rounds of 7-fold cross-validation holding out whole TSS groups). Marginal coverage was nominal in all three datasets (K562 essential development 0.904; RPE1 0.896; K562 genome-wide screen with all K562 essential-library targets removed, "non-library target set", 0.903). Coverage collapsed, however, in the top quintile of measured effect strength (tier T5). It fell to 0.516 in the development set, to 0.480 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.53; 1,751 genes) in the pre-registered RPE1 confirmation, and to 0.518 (95% CI 0.49 to 0.55; 5,683 genes) in the pre-registered confirmation on the non-library target set. Tiers T1–T4 stayed at ≥0.99 throughout (Fig. 1a). In a post hoc illustrative analysis outside the pre-registered protocol, we re-tiered the non-library target set perturbations by effect strength, correcting for a cell-number-matched noise floor estimated from non-targeting control cells. This gave a T5 coverage of 0.664 (95% CI 0.64 to 0.69) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The same pattern held across a second cell line, a disjoint set of target genes, a mean-response model, and a GO-embedding linear model. Under M0, RPE1 T5 coverage was 0.493 and 0.504; on the non-library target set at nominal α it was 0.520 and 0.517. It also held on the non-library target set for k = 20 and 200 (0.519, 0.509) and under split-half tier assignment (0.507).

**This pattern is largely fixed by the quantile construction.** If the score is a strictly increasing function of the stratifying variable, split-conformal sets at 1 − α = 0.9 cover every unit below the 90th percentile and none above it. In effect-strength quintiles, this means T1–T4 are covered with probability 1 and T5 with probability one half (Methods, *Constructive reference*). A Monte Carlo simulation using the actual calibration and test sizes gives 0.517, 0.508 and 0.504 (development, RPE1, non-library target set; archived script, Methods). The mean-response model is an exact instance of this case, because its score equals measured effect strength. Its T5 coverage was 0.511, 0.493 and 0.520. Observed T5 coverage of linear-PCA and linear-GO differed from the mean-model realisation of this reference by −1.3 to +1.5 percentage points, and from the simulated reference by −2.8 to +1.4 percentage points, in all three datasets (Supplementary Table S4). The largest deviation occurred for linear-PCA on RPE1 (T5 coverage 0.480, simulated reference 0.508). This lies well inside the split-to-split spread of the reference (SD of single-split T5 coverage 10.8 percentage points in the simulation). For the primary model in each dataset, the 95% confidence interval of the pooled T5 coverage contains the simulated reference (development 0.47–0.56, RPE1 0.43–0.53, non-library target set 0.49–0.55). The near-complete coverage of T1–T4 is therefore a necessary consequence of the same construction rather than evidence that weak perturbations are well calibrated. What remains informative is how far a predictor's score departs from being a function of effect strength, which we quantify below. Effect-size dependence of prediction difficulty itself is known (Li et al.[5]). Our point concerns how stratified coverage should be read.

## Similarity- and model-uncertainty-based difficulty proxies did not improve top-quintile coverage

Under randomised width matching to M0 (same expected mean interval width), none of six deployable distance- or uncertainty-based proxies improved T5 coverage in either confirmation set (Fig. 1b). The following values report RPE1 and the non-library target set, respectively. For similarity/training-quality difficulty, the normalised version shifted coverage by +1.7 pp (95% CI −0.1 to 3.6) and +0.3 pp (95% CI −0.4 to 1.0); its Mondrian version shifted coverage by −1.2 pp (95% CI −4.1 to 1.6) and −0.8 pp (95% CI −1.9 to 0.4). The Mondrian proxy on raw GO-distance quintiles shifted coverage by −1.1 pp (95% CI −3.7 to 1.4) and −1.3 pp (95% CI −2.6 to −0.1). The Mondrian proxy on STRING-distance quintiles shifted coverage by +0.1 pp (95% CI −3.5 to 3.7) and −0.7 pp (95% CI −2.1 to 0.7). For the PRESCRIBE-like bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy, the normalised version shifted coverage by −4.6 pp (95% CI −9.7 to 0.6) and −10.5 pp (95% CI −13.4 to −7.7); its Mondrian version shifted coverage by −1.8 pp (95% CI −3.4 to −0.2) and −0.6 pp (95% CI −1.6 to 0.4). The development-set advantage of the Mondrian similarity proxy (+5.7 pp) did not replicate. Across all three datasets, after adjusting for noise, cell number and other covariates, distance terms showed no robust association with miscoverage for linear-PCA. For linear-GO, after the same adjustment, GO distance retained a modest positive association on the development set and RPE1, but not on the non-library target set (descriptive).

## On the three screens the score retains little variation independent of effect scale (R ≤ 0.193)

**Heterogeneous coverage is heterogeneous scale (descriptive).** The conformity score is close to proportional to measured effect strength in all three screens, which leaves little room for a scale-independent share R. The ratio S1/E_true has a coefficient of variation of 0.107 (development), 0.097 (RPE1) and 0.008 (non-library target set). Spearman correlations between S1 and effect strength are 0.94, 0.96 and 1.00; for the mean-response model the two are identical by construction. The fitted models remove only 4.3%, 4.3% and 0.1% of the mean-model error in the three datasets (linear-GO: 8.5%, 6.5% and 0.2%). So the score largely reflects the perturbation's actual scale rather than model-specific residual (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Note 1 S5). Normalising the score by the measured effect strength is a reference method, not width-matched; realised mean width is 0.67–1.00 of M0 in 20 of 21 RPE1 splits and 0.65–0.75 in 21 of 21 non-library-target-set splits (Supplementary Table S2). This normalisation brings T5 coverage to 0.998 / 0.999 / 0.988 (development / RPE1 / non-library target set) by dividing out the tier-defining scale. We summarise the model-specific departure from this scale coupling by R, the share of the variance of log S1 not explained by log effect strength. R = 0 for the mean model and R = 0.065 for linear-PCA on RPE1 (development 0.112; Supplementary Table S4).

## The non-linear model GEARS improved point accuracy without detectably reducing score–scale coupling (post-confirmation)

Training the non-linear model GEARS on RPE1 under a frozen protocol (post-confirmation tier; Methods) improved point prediction accuracy over linear-PCA. Yet we detected no reduction in score–scale coupling. In this test, the accuracy gain did not make effect-stratified coverage more informative.

On the 21 splits with the same S1, references and test units, GEARS removed 6.3% of the mean-model error overall and 9.7% in T5. Linear-PCA removed 4.3% and 7.9% (paired differences +1.9 percentage points, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.4, and +1.8, 95% CI 0.8 to 2.8). However, its scale-independent share was R = 0.059 versus 0.065 for linear-PCA, a ratio of 0.91 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.11). The coefficient of variation of S1/E_true was 0.112 versus 0.097 (ratio 1.16, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.27; Supplementary Table S8).

Under the pre-specified rule this is classified as "not reduced": we detected no reduction of the coupling. This is an absence of evidence for a reduction, as the R ratio interval includes 1 and spans roughly 24% downwards to 11% upwards. Split-conformal T5 coverage with GEARS scores remained at 0.498 against the analytic reference of 0.5. T1–T4 coverage held at 1.000, with marginal coverage at 0.899.

Three controls were frozen before any GEARS output was read, to calibrate this result (Methods). The blank control (mean model) has R = 0 by construction. The positive control is not deployable: it predicts each perturbation's half-B profile from its own half-A cells. On the half-data basis it reached R = 0.830 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.87), compared with 0.039 for linear-PCA and 0.036 for GEARS. It also substantially outperformed linear-PCA in accuracy (paired RER difference +28.9 percentage points overall, 95% CI 27.5 to 30.3; +44.9 in T5; development: R = 0.980 versus 0.104). Its pre-specified verdict is "threshold reachable": in these data the coupling can be broken by a predictor that tracks each perturbation's own response. This control has all perturbation-specific signal and is limited only by the measurement noise of half the cells. It therefore shows that the threshold is attainable, not which signal GEARS misses. In the negative control (permuted predictions among test perturbations), all 20 permutations of linear-PCA and 19 of 20 permutations of GEARS were classified "not reduced" (the remaining GEARS permutation was "coupling metrics up with accuracy down"). So the decision rule did not mistake added, perturbation-unrelated error for decoupling.

## On combinatorial perturbations with measured single-gene components, R exceeds the diagnostic threshold

On combinatorial CRISPRa perturbations whose single-gene components had been measured in training, both an additive baseline and linear-PCA achieved large error reductions. Both also elevated R well above the pre-specified diagnostic threshold, the regime in which effect-stratified coverage can reflect model differences. Across the 129 gene pairs of the Norman screen (387 test predictions over 21 splits), the additive baseline reduced the mean-model error by 0.498 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.54) and left a share R = 0.662 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.78) of its score unexplained by effect strength. Linear-PCA reduced the error by 0.339 (95% CI 0.30 to 0.38) with R = 0.486 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.60). Both exceeded the pre-specified threshold of 0.224, with lower bounds above 0.112, earning the pre-specified classification "R clearly elevated" (Fig. 2a).

The negative controls behaved as specified. With effect strength permuted, R approached one for every model (0.996 additive, 0.996 linear-PCA, 0.997 mean model), and the T5 − T1 coverage difference vanished (−0.2, −0.7 and −0.7 pp). With predictions permuted, R rose (0.465 additive, 0.314 linear-PCA), but error increased. So all 20 permutations of each model were classified "R elevated without accuracy gain," and none as clearly elevated. The split-half predictor reached R = 0.818 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.94).

The mean-response model again realised the constructive reference, with marginal coverage of 0.897 and T5 coverage of 0.532 (95% CI 0.39 to 0.67). Even so, the large R values let point estimates of T5 coverage separate by model. The additive baseline reached 0.722 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.85); linear-PCA reached 0.430 (95% CI 0.29 to 0.59). Marginal coverages were 0.904 and 0.868, both still below nominal in T5. Relative to the mean model on the same test rows, T5 coverage differed by +19.0 pp for the additive baseline and by −10.1 pp for linear-PCA (Fig. 2b). On the three single-gene screens, the difference was ≤1.5 pp (Supplementary Table S4). Gene-level dependence across the 129 pairs (formed from 71 genes, with one gene in 12 pairs) was accounted for via two-way dyadic gene resampling (post hoc; protocol v1.3, frozen before computation; Methods). This widened the R intervals, but both models still satisfied every condition of the pre-specified elevation rule (additive R 95% CI 0.44 to 0.86; linear-PCA 0.27 to 0.69). By contrast, the T5 coverage differences under gene resampling were no longer distinguishable from zero (additive 95% CI −18.8 to 51.9 pp; linear-PCA −47.2 to 26.7 pp; pair-level intervals 0.0 to 38.0 and −28.6 to 8.3 pp; Supplementary Table S9). The elevation of R is thus robust to gene-level dependence. The coverage shift, however, is a direction in the point estimates rather than an established difference.

**Which pairs carry the elevation (post hoc).** We repeated the analysis across the three generalization classes of GEARS[3], defined by how many genes of a pair were perturbed alone in training (protocol v1.3; 71 genes split into five folds, three repeats; two-way gene-resampling intervals in Supplementary Table S10). The elevation of R is consistent with dependence on measured single-gene components.

With one gene of a pair held out (129 pairs, 618 test predictions), linear-PCA retained an error reduction of 0.188 (95% CI 0.11 to 0.28) and R = 0.442 (95% CI 0.24 to 0.70). Predicting each pair by its measured single-gene component alone gave R = 0.468 (95% CI 0.29 to 0.62) and reduced error by 0.267 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.33, versus 0.188, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.28 for linear-PCA; paired difference not tested under the protocol).

With both genes held out (63 pairs, 78 test predictions), linear-PCA showed no measurable accuracy gain over the mean-response model (error reduction −0.057, 95% CI −0.24 to 0.04; with K = 10, error reduction −0.007, 95% CI −0.16 to 0.11). Its R of 0.302 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.83) was classified as uncertain under the pre-written rule, where without an accuracy gain, R does not indicate perturbation-specific signal.

These results are consistent with the elevation of R depending on the single-gene perturbations of the components having been measured. The linear model showed no measurable gain beyond the measured component.

## The diagnostic R stays small in HepG2 and Jurkat single-gene screens, as predicted

We applied the same audit design to essential-library CRISPRi screens in HepG2 and Jurkat cells[18]. The protocol was frozen before the data were read (post-confirmation), covering 1,632 and 1,908 evaluated target genes across 21 splits. The pre-specified prediction that linear-PCA would not raise R held in both cell lines. R remained well below the 0.224 threshold in both: 0.086 (95% CI 0.07 to 0.11) in HepG2 and 0.038 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.05) in Jurkat, with error reductions of 0.051 and 0.023, respectively. As on the three original screens, top-quintile coverage stayed at its constructive value. In HepG2 it was 0.532 versus 0.531 for the mean-response model (difference 0.1 pp, 95% CI −3.9 to 4.2); in Jurkat it was 0.515 versus 0.521 (−0.6 pp, 95% CI −3.2 to 2.0). Both negative controls passed. The split-half positive control reached R = 0.943 and 0.702, so the threshold was attainable on these data (Supplementary Table S11).

## A prior-neighbour proxy for effect strength passed a pre-registered transfer test across cell lines but not on a disjoint gene set

The score sits close to a multiple of effect strength. A deployable method that changes stratified coverage therefore needs information about each perturbation's scale before the experiment. We found that a deployable prior-neighbour proxy (ĥ, built from GO and STRING neighbours' training-set responses) passed our pre-registered transfer test across cell lines within essential genes (RPE1). It did not transfer to the disjoint non-library target set. Both outcomes are in line with the proxy's out-of-sample predictability on each set.

*Essential-gene screen (RPE1; cross-cell-line).* On RPE1, ĥ predicted effect strength out of sample (Spearman ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.47; AUROC for T5 0.74). Normalising the conformity score by ĥ (M1) raised width-matched T5 coverage by +9.7 pp (95% CI 5.8 to 13.6; Fig. 1c; development: +12.0 pp, 95% CI 8.0 to 16.0). This passed both pre-registered hierarchical steps (step 1 vs M0; step 2 vs the strongest development-set baseline Mdist-M, +10.9 pp, 95% CI 7.8 to 14.0; all guards satisfied). Even so, M1 T5 coverage remained partial at 0.577, and T4 coverage decreased from 1.000 to 0.953. In the decision scenario (top 10% by ĥ), coverage rose by +17.4 pp (95% CI 12.6 to 22.3) with a subset width ratio of 1.38. Because the subset is selected by the same ĥ that allocates width, this endpoint shows reallocation of a fixed total width toward perturbations ranked high by ĥ. Fitting a score directly to the same features (Mscore-N; +11.9 pp, 95% CI 7.8 to 16.0) performed similarly. This is consistent with development-set feature comparisons placing most of ĥ's signal in prior-neighbour features (descriptive; Supplementary Note 1). Results on RPE1 were consistent under grid-based width matching, k = 20 and 200, split-half tier assignment and linear-GO.

*Non-library target set (cross-gene-set).* On the non-library target set, ĥ barely predicted effect strength (ρ = 0.11, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.14; AUROC 0.57, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.59; class P− under the pre-specified rule). Pre-registered step 1 was therefore not passed ("step 1 not passed: repair not confirmed"). M1 changed width-matched T5 coverage by +1.0 pp (95% CI −0.1 to 2.1; pre-specified margin 5 pp) and Mscore-N by +0.7 pp (95% CI −0.5 to 1.8). Statistical power was high even at half the development-set effect: post hoc scenario power was 0.960 at the observed SE of 0.6 pp, and pre-specified C0 power was 0.809 (the CI is consistent with true effects up to about 2 pp). In the decision scenario, coverage rose by +4.4 pp (95% CI 3.1 to 5.7) with a subset width ratio of 1.09. All other guards were met: nominal marginal coverage was 0.903, and the permuted-ĥ control had a mean of −0.9 pp, with 0/20 permutations passing step 1. Grid-based width matching gave the same outcome (+0.8 pp, 95% CI −0.3 to 1.9), as did linear-GO (width-matched T5 coverage 0.517 under M0 and 0.526 under M1).

Prospectively identifiable subsets (the 10% of test perturbations with the highest ĥ) also showed under-coverage under M0. Coverage was 0.740, 0.708 and 0.822 for the development, RPE1, and non-library target sets, respectively. These deficits agreed within 3.1 percentage points with predictions from each subset's share of T5 units alone (Supplementary Table S12). This agreement is what the quantile construction predicts, so it adds no evidence beyond the construction (post-confirmation, descriptive).

## Network modularity and complex membership are associated with where prior-neighbour predictions succeed (post-confirmation exploratory)

In post-confirmation exploratory analyses (protocol frozen before running; Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. S3), we found that effect strength is highly repeatable across cell halves in all three datasets (Spearman–Brown reliability 0.99). It is also strongly network-autocorrelated among essential-library targets, but weakly autocorrelated among non-library targets (STRING neighbour correlation 0.41 vs 0.11). This pattern is in line with the proxy transferring across cell lines within essential genes but not to the non-library target set. Attenuation correction or a cross-split definition of effect strength (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left ĥ's predictability on the non-library target set essentially unchanged (ρ = 0.11 and 0.12). Outcome measurement noise therefore does not account for the low predictability. Within each dataset, ĥ predicted effect strength better for CORUM complex members and highly connected genes (offered as a hypothesis in *Discussion*, given that ĥ is built from neighbours' effect strengths). Under M0, coverage of CORUM complex members (0.886, 0.890, 0.892) was fully accounted for by their T5 share (Supplementary Table S13). Under M0, highly connected genes, however, were covered somewhat better than their T5 share predicts on the two essential-gene screens but not on the non-library target set (recorded as a post-unblinding hypothesis; Supplementary Note 1 S6).

## Effect strength retains its association with miscoverage after adjustment for noise, cell number and network covariates (descriptive)

In a descriptive logistic model of M0 miscoverage, log effect strength retained a strong positive association even after adjusting for split-half noise and reliability, cell number, GO and STRING distance, target baseline expression, GO similarity, and shared neighbours. The log-odds per SD of log effect strength went from 4.50 to 3.63 on RPE1 and from 5.30 to 5.21 on the development set (split-half version). It was at least as strong on the non-library target set: split-half version +7.44 unadjusted (95% CI 6.78 to 8.23) and +5.52 adjusted (95% CI 4.21 to 6.77); the full version was quasi-separated, so only the sign from the Firth bootstrap is reported. Target baseline expression was negatively associated with miscoverage across all three datasets (non-library target set, split-half −0.35, 95% CI −0.54 to −0.19), though the mechanism is not established. Noise and cell number were collinear, and we do not read changes in non-collapsible logistic coefficients as shares of confounding.

## Persistently miscovered perturbations are high-effect genes enriched for RNA-processing and ribosomal terms (development set, descriptive)

Descriptively, in the development set, perturbations persistently miscovered across models were essentially the strongest-effect perturbations. Of 93 core genes, 78 were among the 93 highest-effect genes. These persistently miscovered perturbations were also enriched for RNA exosome, nuclear RNA decay, RNA 3′-end processing, RNA polymerase II/TFIID, ribosome and rRNA processing terms. Enriched terms centred on RNA exosome, nuclear RNA decay, RNA 3′-end processing, RNA polymerase II/TFIID, ribosome and rRNA processing. Strong-effect (T5) targets were more often CORUM complex members (78% vs 64%; OR 1.96, p = 6×10⁻⁷) and had more STRING partners. After adjusting for STRING degree and GO annotation count, complex membership was no longer independently associated (+0.10 log-odds per SD, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.24; see *Limitations* regarding STRING/CORUM overlap and study bias). This relates to where the proxy is predictable (post-confirmation exploratory).

# Discussion


**What stratified coverage measures.** Effect-stratified coverage of single-cell perturbation predictions is largely fixed by the quantile construction whenever the conformity score is close to a multiple of effect strength. The scale-independent score share R and its constructive reference indicate when stratified coverage reflects the model rather than this construction.

Across three Perturb-seq screens, marginally valid split-conformal prediction sets for linear perturbation models covered only about half of the 20% strongest perturbations. They covered almost all of the weaker ones. Coverage stayed within 1.5 percentage points of the mean-model constructive reference (within 2.8 points of its Monte Carlo simulation).

When a predictor captures little perturbation-specific signal beyond overall response size, the conformity score is close to a multiple of the perturbation's effect strength. When that is so, a single global quantile covers T1–T4 with probability close to 1 and T5 with probability close to one half.

This bears directly on the recommendation of Li et al.[5] to evaluate perturbation models stratified by effect size. Such stratification is useful, but when a predictor captures little specific signal, effect-stratified coverage is largely determined by construction. The constructive reference and R should therefore be reported alongside stratified coverage to isolate the model-specific component—especially in the top-effect regime that practitioners prioritise.

**A more accurate non-linear model did not reduce the coupling detectably.** GEARS reduced the mean-model error more than linear-PCA overall and in the top tier. Yet its scale-independent share R was not larger (ratio 0.91, 95% CI 0.76 to 1.11), and its T5 coverage was 0.498, at the constructive reference. This is an absence of evidence for a reduction, with an interval that includes no change; it is not evidence that the coupling cannot be reduced. The split-half positive control shows that breaking the coupling is attainable in principle. Predicting each perturbation's profile from its own other half of the cells gave R = 0.830 on the half-data basis, against 0.039 for linear-PCA and 0.036 for GEARS. That control uses the perturbation's own measurements, so it is out of reach for any prediction of an unseen perturbation. It marks how much scale-independent variation a measurement-noise-limited predictor with full specific signal would show. The gap between it and both models thus quantifies how far current predictors are from that level, not which signal GEARS misses. The negative control confirms that the decision rule does not mistake perturbation-unrelated error for decoupling (20/20 linear-PCA and 19/20 GEARS permutations "not reduced"). Outside the pre-specified analyses, GEARS predictions permuted among perturbations still reduced the mean-model error by 3.4% (linear-PCA 0.6%). This suggests that part of GEARS's accuracy gain is not perturbation-specific.

**When stratified coverage is informative.** The combinatorial screen supplies the regime in which the diagnostic departs from zero. Both genes of each held-out pair had been measured alone. Because of this, the additive baseline and the linear model captured much of each pair's specific response, raising R to 0.662 and 0.486. Effect-stratified coverage could then reflect the models rather than the score alone. Point estimates of top-quintile coverage shifted in the direction of model accuracy. Under gene-level resampling, however, their intervals included the constructive reference (27 pairs in T5). The unseen-component ablation is consistent with the elevation of R depending on single-gene components having been measured. When both genes of a pair were held out, the linear model showed no measurable accuracy gain over the mean-response model, and R was uncertain under the pre-written rule. This parallels GEARS's own stratified evaluation on this screen[3]. The result comes from a single screen in one laboratory. It shows that R can be large in real data and that the reading rule then changes, not how often this regime occurs. We did not run GEARS on this screen. Whether a model with prior-knowledge structure retains an accuracy gain, and with it a large R, for fully unseen pairs is the natural next test. By contrast, in two further single-gene CRISPRi screens from the same laboratory and platform[18], R stayed below the pre-specified threshold as predicted (HepG2 0.086, Jurkat 0.038). This supports a small R as the typical state of single-gene CRISPRi screens with current linear models across cell types, but not, as tested here, across laboratories.

**Distance-based proxies did not capture this axis.** Out-of-distribution work on perturbation prediction emphasises distance from the training data. Yet six deployable distance- and uncertainty-based proxies—GO and STRING distances, similarity- and data-quality-based difficulty, and a PRESCRIBE-like bootstrap model-uncertainty proxy—produced no T5 gain on either confirmation set under width matching. They also showed no residual association with miscoverage for the primary model once effect strength was accounted for. Effect strength, by contrast, remained the dominant predictor of miscoverage in every dataset. These are six specific proxies, one score and two linear models, so the finding is that distance did not capture the failure axis here, not that distance is irrelevant in general. We suggest that calibration audits of perturbation models report coverage stratified by effect strength alongside similarity-based OOD splits. This would extend effect-size-stratified accuracy benchmarking[5] (whose direction of dependence varied with the metric) to uncertainty quantification.

**A prior-neighbour proxy transferred across cell lines but not across gene sets.** Because the score tracks effect strength, a deployable method can change stratified coverage only by using pre-experiment information about each perturbation's scale. Our prior-neighbour proxy ĥ passed the pre-registered transfer test on RPE1 (E1 +9.7 pp, 95% CI 5.8 to 13.6). It failed the test on the non-library target set (E1 +1.0 pp, 95% CI −0.1 to 2.1; post hoc scenario power 0.960 for half the development-set effect at the observed SE). This split is in line with its predictability (ρ = 0.43 vs 0.11). Subsets defined by the proxy before the experiment under-covered by the amount their effect-strength composition predicts. They add no evidence beyond the construction. Post-confirmation analyses point to network modularity. Effect strength was network-autocorrelated on STRING among essential genes (0.41 vs 0.11 among non-library targets), and highly connected essential genes were covered somewhat better than their T5 composition predicts. This is consistent with the possibility that essential genes act through dense, co-regulated complexes whose partners predict effect scale. That idea is a hypothesis generated after unblinding on subsets we chose, not a finding.

**The repair has a cost.** Normalising by ĥ at equal total width reallocates interval width from perturbations predicted to be weak to those predicted to be strong. This reduces T5 coverage for perturbations with sparse prior information. In the lowest STRING-degree tertile, the M1 T5 coverage change was negative in all three datasets. CIs excluded zero on RPE1 (−14.9 pp, 95% CI −22.4 to −7.5) and the non-library target set (−3.7 pp, 95% CI −5.5 to −1.8; development −5.1 pp, 95% CI −15.8 to 5.5). Gains, by contrast, concentrated in densely connected genes (non-library target set, high-degree stratum at fixed cut-points, +10.2 pp, 95% CI 6.0 to 14.3; descriptive, uncorrected across nine groups). Practitioners should therefore not apply prior-neighbour normalisation uniformly. Gating the repair by expected proxy predictability, such as network degree, is a natural extension. We did not construct or test this on the present data, because any rule tuned here would need new data for a valid confirmation.

## Limitations


1. *Circularity.* Because ĥ is built from neighbours' effect strengths, low network autocorrelation and low ĥ predictability overlap conceptually; however, high split-half (≈ 0.99) and cross-split reliability of effect strength, consistent within-dataset stratification by network degree and complex membership across all three datasets, and a persistent cross-dataset gap within matched degree strata provide non-circular evidence (though split-half reliability counts any component shared by both cell halves as signal).
2. *Reliability is not validity.* Split-half reliability counts components shared by both halves (such as a cell-number-dependent noise floor in top-gene selection, or batch) as signal. Although an illustrative cross-split analysis (genes selected on one half, magnitude measured on the other) left ĥ's predictability unchanged (non-library target set, ρ = 0.12) and partial reliability given cell number stayed high (0.960), measured effect strength on the non-library target set is strongly anti-correlated with cell number (ρ = −0.80), so part of the strong-effect tier there may reflect measurement precision rather than biological effect size; this cannot be separated from fitness effects on cell yield with the present data (Supplementary Note 1 S3). In a post hoc illustrative analysis, re-tiering perturbations by effect strength corrected for a cell-number-matched control-cell null left the failure essentially unchanged on both essential-gene screens (M0 T5 coverage 0.552 and 0.492) but raised T5 coverage on the non-library target set from 0.518 to 0.664 (where 21% of perturbations did not exceed the null), indicating that low-cell-number imprecision accounts for a sizeable part of the apparent failure there; noise-corrected coverage should accompany such audits.
3. *Study bias.* STRING degree and CORUM membership partly reflect how intensively a gene has been studied rather than biological modularity alone, and our analyses cannot separate the two.
4. *Selection of evaluation units and asymmetric cell-count floor.* Excluding perturbations with <30 cells from evaluation removes targets with systematically higher measured effect strength (medians 7.9 vs 3.6, 6.6 vs 3.9 and 9.6 vs 3.2; 73–100% above the included T5 cut-point) but lower measurement precision, truncating the strongest tail with an unsigned bias on coverage. Conversely, among kept units, the ≥30-cell floor applies to the reference and mean model (`pipeline_v05.MIN_CELLS_REF=30`) but not to the linear models' fitting columns or the proxy's neighbour features, making the proxy's input noisier than the reference; weighting or filtering neighbour features by cell count was not run and remains future work.
5. *Scope of confirmation and proxy repair.* All three primary screens come from one laboratory and platform (two in K562), and 82% of RPE1 targets overlap the development set, so RPE1 tests generalisation across cell lines rather than across genes, whereas the non-library target set tests generalisation to new genes; cross-laboratory replication[18] is needed. On RPE1, step 2 passed partly because the comparator (Mdist-M) failed to replicate, the M1 repair was partial (T5 coverage 0.577, T4 reduced from 1.000 to 0.953), and the similarity of M1 and Mscore-N does not by itself attribute the gain to a specific feature group.
6. *Hyper-parameter grids.* Selected K and d often hit the upper grid boundary; an exploratory extension on RPE1 (K up to 200, d up to 200) left the failure and partial repair unchanged (M0 T5 0.491; M1 − M0 +9.5 pp, 95% CI 5.6 to 13.4), though d still sat at the ceiling in every split with < 2% validation error improvement (Supplementary Note 1 S4).
7. *Choices of score, unit and difficulty proxies.* Results apply to a perturbation-level score (S1) on perturbation-specific genes relative to the training centroid and six specific distance/uncertainty proxies with two linear models (where the bootstrap proxy is a PRESCRIBE-like linear implementation, not PRESCRIBE itself); per-gene intervals, differential-expression calls or other distance proxies may behave differently.
8. *Single-model, single-dataset scope of the GEARS test.* The GEARS test covers one non-linear model (GEARS v0.1.2, default settings) on RPE1 only, with training data capped at 16 cells per perturbation and 15 out-of-graph always-train perturbations excluded (which may lower accuracy relative to using all cells), and the split-half positive control uses the perturbation's own cells (not deployable) and does not identify which signal GEARS misses; "no detected reduction" does not rule out decoupling by other or larger models, or by GEARS on combinatorial screens (not run here).
9. *Deviations in the GEARS run (Methods; Supplementary Note 2).* The run used (a) an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU instead of the protocol-specified A800; (b) a violation of the stop-on-error rule after out-of-memory failures, with all execution changes moved into five runtime patches outside the restored, checksum-verified frozen code, whose equivalence tests were run by the executing agent and not repeated by us; (c) a one-epoch debug run in a separate directory; (d) co-expression graphs not compared edge by edge across machines, with some correlations within 3 × 10⁻⁵ of the threshold; and (e) unquantified GPU non-determinism (plus three log details not confirmable from the records). No output of a failed or debug attempt entered the results and no result was selected among attempts.
10. *Post-confirmation status of the coupling analyses.* The constructive reference, the scale-independent share R, the GEARS test and its controls were defined after the confirmations had been observed and are interpretive and post-confirmation, not confirmatory.
11. *Combinatorial perturbations and excluded screens.* The combinatorial analysis rests on one screen (Norman et al.) from one cell line and laboratory (129 evaluated pairs, 27 in T5; 63 pairs in the two-unseen-genes ablation; two pairs with absent target names C19orf26, C3orf72, KIAA1804 excluded without synonym mapping; pair embedding defined as the sum of gene embeddings with K at the grid ceiling in every split). Because 71 genes form the 129 pairs (one gene in 12 pairs), pre-specified pair-level intervals ignore gene-sharing correlation, while post hoc two-way gene resampling (whose vertex bootstrap product weights can be conservative) preserves the R verdict but widens T5 coverage-difference intervals to include zero. A second combinatorial screen (Cas13d RNA knockdown[16]) passed paper-level gates but failed data-level applicability gates under protocol v1.4 (only 136 of 157 pairs with ≥30 cells, 86.6%, had both single-gene perturbations measured with ≥30 cells against a 90% gate—shortfall involving MYB, 15 cells, and MED13L, 27 cells—and 424 control cells against a 500 gate) and was not analysed as pre-specified; a transcription-factor overexpression screen[17] had 44 pairs (below the 60-pair gate; Supplementary Table S14).
12. *Further cell lines.* HepG2 and Jurkat share the laboratory, library and platform of the original screens (testing cell type rather than laboratory), were read from scPerturb copies under protocol amendment v1.2.1 after five data checks passed, using `ncounts` library size (~1% smaller than transcriptome-wide `UMI_count`, median ratio 1.01), and selected the largest K on the grid in 21 of 21 HepG2 and 18 of 21 Jurkat splits.

# Conclusions

Under current perturbation-response predictors, coverage stratified by measured effect strength is largely determined by the quantile construction. This is because the conformity score is close to a multiple of effect scale. Audits of uncertainty for perturbation models should report the constructive reference and the scale-independent share R alongside stratified coverage. In our single-gene data, neither a linear nor a non-linear predictor departed detectably from the constructive case. A measurement-limited control, however, shows that departure is possible. A prior-network proxy for scale transferred across cell lines within essential genes, but not to a set of targets outside that library.

# Methods


**Evidence tiers.** Results are labelled as **confirmatory** (a pre-registered endpoint of a protocol frozen with SHA-256 manifests before any response of that confirmation set entered a score, computed once in the sense defined under *What was computed when*), **descriptive** (a pre-specified quantity without a pre-registered decision rule), **post-confirmation exploratory** (its own protocol frozen before running, but after the confirmations were unblinded) or **post hoc illustrative** (outside every protocol, added in response to a specific question). No confirmatory configuration, criterion or classification was changed after it was observed; a complete freeze timeline, the time each dataset's responses were initially read, and every post-freeze deviation with its nature and validation are given in Supplementary Table S1.

**Rounding.** All reported numbers are rounded half away from zero, directly from the unrounded values in the archived result files. Coverages, power and other proportions (including the shares R and coefficients of variation) are given to three decimals; percentage-point differences, percentages and their confidence intervals to one decimal; ratios, correlations, AUROCs, log-odds coefficients and the confidence intervals of proportions and ratios to two decimals. Protocol parameters, thresholds and grid values are quoted as specified; P values are given to one significant figure. Ranges are formed from rounded endpoints.

## Data

We used the genome-scale CRISPRi Perturb-seq screens of Replogle et al.[1] (raw single-cell and bulk files, Figshare+ doi:10.25452/figshare.plus.20029387.v1): K562 essential-gene screen (development set), RPE1 essential-gene screen (primary confirmation set) and the K562 genome-wide screen (secondary confirmation set) from which all 2,057 targets of the K562 essential-gene screen were removed before any other processing step, so that no such target enters any fold including training; we call the remainder the "non-library target set" (defined by absence from that screening library rather than biological non-essentiality). File sizes and checksums were verified against the publisher's records (computed MD5 where provided, uploader-supplied MD5 otherwise) and SHA-256 values were recorded. For each perturbation we formed a pseudobulk profile as the mean over cells of log1p(counts per 10,000) and, for split-half analyses, two pseudobulks from a random partition of its cells into halves A and B (fixed seed). Single-cell pseudobulks were checked against the publisher's bulk files (per-perturbation mean UMI; non-library target set maximum absolute difference 2.4 × 10⁻⁵, zero elements outside tolerance).

**Units and leakage control.** The exchangeable unit is the target gene. Evaluation units ("E units") were targets with at least 30 cells that met the pre-specified inclusion rules; targets sharing a transcription start site within 1 kb (Ensembl GRCh38 coordinates) were merged into TSS groups, because CRISPRi guides can repress neighbouring promoters. All splits assign whole TSS groups; non-E perturbations were always placed in training ("always-train") unless they shared a TSS group with an E unit, in which case they were dropped. Sizes: development 1,786 E units (1,721 TSS groups); RPE1 1,751 genes evaluated; non-library target set 5,683 E units (5,449 TSS groups) plus 2,001 always-train units.

**Cell-count floor.** Evaluation units require ≥30 cells. Inside a split, the reference quantities μ_tr and σ_g — and therefore the mean-response model, whose prediction is exactly μ_tr — are computed only from training-pool units with ≥30 cells (`pipeline_v05.MIN_CELLS_REF=30`, asserted at run time). The linear models' fitting columns and the neighbour features of the deployable proxies (ĥ's GO- and STRING-weighted neighbour effect strength, and the neighbour cell-count/noise features) carry no cell-count floor, so responses measured in fewer than 30 cells enter the gene embeddings, fitted coefficients and neighbour averages (see *Limitations*).

**Splits.** Repeated grouped 7-fold: in each of 3 rounds (seed = base + round), TSS groups were dealt to 7 balanced test folds; for each test fold the remaining units were split by TSS group into validation (10% of E), calibration (15%) and training (rest), giving 21 splits in which every E unit is tested once per round. Split files were written once, set read-only, and verified by hash before every run.

## Perturbation-response models

Three models were trained on the training pool (E training units plus always-train units): (i) the **mean model**, predicting the training-reference centroid; (ii) **linear-PCA**, the linear baseline of Ahlmann-Eltze et al.[2] (gene embedding from the top K principal components of the training expression matrix, two-sided ridge λ = 0.1), with K selected on the validation fold from {5, 10, 20, 30, 50}; and (iii) **linear-GO**, the same model with perturbation embeddings from a d-dimensional adjacency spectral embedding of the GEARS Gene Ontology graph[3], d ∈ {2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 100}. linear-PCA with k = 50 was the pre-specified primary model. Deep model evaluation was conducted in a post-confirmation protocol with GEARS (below).

**Reference and score.** Following Systema[4], expression was referenced to the centroid of training perturbations rather than to control cells: μ is the mean over training perturbations with ≥ 30 cells, σ_g = 1.4826 × MAD_g, floored at the 5th percentile across genes. The nonconformity score S1 of a perturbation is the σ-scaled root-mean-square prediction error over the k = 50 genes with the largest absolute specific effect |y − μ|/σ, excluding readout columns of the target gene (k = 20 and 200 as sensitivity analyses). **Effect strength** E_true is S1 of the mean model, i.e. the σ-scaled RMS specific effect on those genes; tiers T1–T5 are its quintiles over the E units of each dataset.

## Conformal calibration methods

All methods target 1 − α = 0.9 and use the calibration fold of each split.

- **M0:** split-conformal prediction[6] with the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th calibration score.
- **M1 (primary):** normalised score S1/(ĥ + c), c = γ · median(ĥ_OOF), γ = 0 selected on the development set, where ĥ is a deployable prediction of effect strength (below).
- **Distance / difficulty baselines:** Mdist-N and Mdist-M (normalised and Mondrian [3 bins] calibration on a similarity/training-quality difficulty proxy d̂); MD-GO and MD-STR (Mondrian calibration on quintiles of the raw GO-Jaccard or STRING distance to the training pool); Munc-N and Munc-M (a PRESCRIBE-like[8] model-uncertainty proxy: the dispersion of linear-PCA predictions over 50 bootstrap refits of the training pool).
- **Secondary and reference methods:** Mscore-N (all ĥ features fitted directly to the out-of-fold score), M2 (Mondrian on ĥ), M3 (conditional conformal of Gibbs, Cherian and Candès[7], own exact linear-programming implementation cross-checked against the authors' package), a **reference method** that normalises by the measured E_true (not deployable, and partly definitional: for the mean-response model the score equals E_true exactly, so the normalised score is constant, and on the non-library target set the normalised score of linear-PCA is nearly constant, coefficient of variation 0.008) and **M_perm** (ĥ permuted within calibration and test; 20 permutations; negative control).

**Deployable proxies.** ĥ is a ridge regression (λ chosen by grouped inner cross-validation) of log E_true on prior-only and training-derived features: GO-Jaccard and STRING similarities and distances to the training pool, Jaccard- and STRING-weighted mean effect strengths of the 20 most GO-similar and all STRING neighbours in the training pool, number of GO annotations and STRING degree. The learner is trained on the E training units of the split only (always-train units act as neighbours and in model fitting), with 5 inner TSS-group folds for out-of-fold features. No feature uses the response, cell number or knockdown of a calibration or test unit.

Prediction sets are {y : S(x,y) ≤ t_i}; the reported width is the capped radius (Width matching, below). Every method in Table 1 uses only calibration scores and proxies; no test response enters any threshold.

Key implementation details:

1. **Bin edges and normalisation constants never use calibration or test units.** `mondrian_edges` takes quantiles of the *training out-of-fold* proxy, and c = γ·median of the same out-of-fold vector, with γ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 1} (`methods_v06.mondrian_edges`, `thresholds`).
2. **M3** (`methods_v06.m3_threshold`) implements the randomised conditional set of Gibbs, Cherian & Candès (2025) exactly, as a single linear program: with g(e) = max{Σᵢ ηᵢ Sᵢ : ηᵢ ∈ [−α, 1−α], Σᵢ ηᵢ φᵢ = −e φ_i}, the test dual satisfies {S : η_{n+1}(S) < U} = {S ≤ −g′(U)}, and −g′(U) is read from the equality-constraint marginals of that LP. U_i = u_i − α with u_i ~ Uniform(0,1) fixed per split by seed. Infeasible programs mean U lies outside the attainable range of η_{n+1} and give ±∞ (all / empty); two documented numerical fallbacks (interior-point, then bisection on η_{n+1}) compute the same quantity. The implementation was cross-checked against `conditionalconformal` 0.0.5 (protocol v0.6.1 S11c).
3. **σ̂_boot** (`ext_v07dev.compute_ext`, `_unc_from_moments`): B = 50 refits of linear-PCA on TSS-group bootstrap resamples of the training pool; per unit, the σ-scaled prediction dispersion (variance with ddof = 1) averaged over the 50 genes with the largest |mean prediction|, target column(s) excluded. Out-of-fold values for training units use only refits in which the unit was out of bag (minimum 3).
4. **D_GO, D_STRING** (`pipeline_v05.distances`): prior-only distances from the query unit to the training pool, identical to the covariates used elsewhere (asserted against the stage-1 cache at run time); out-of-fold versions for training units use the inner folds.
5. **M_perm** (`methods_v06.permute_within`, `eval_v06.N_PERM = 20`): the proxy is permuted within calibration and within test separately, 20 permutations with fixed seeds; the decision subset is always selected with the real ĥ, so the control changes only the calibration, not the subset.
6. **Width matching** (`confirm_v07.rand_rows`): Because conditional-coverage gains can be bought with wider sets, methods were compared at equal mean width. For each split, the level α′ of each method was chosen on the grid 0.010–0.400 (0.01–0.40, step 0.005) using only calibration scores, proxies and the matching target, and mixed between the two bracketing grid points with a per-unit uniform draw (probability π) so that the expected mean capped width equals M0's at α = 0.1 (primary, "randomised matching"; nearest-grid matching as sensitivity); test responses enter solely in the coverage indicator. Interval width is the capped radius of the score ball (+∞ replaced by twice the largest calibration score, empty sets counted as 0), fixed in the protocol to accommodate infinite conditional-conformal thresholds. After matching, the nominal level is α′ (on RPE1, M1 matched at α′ between 0.060 and 0.115), so matching is an **empirical equal-width comparison** rather than a finite-sample guarantee at 1 − 0.1, and marginal coverage after matching is reported as a measured quantity. Statuses `unmatched_too_narrow` / `unmatched_too_wide` record methods whose width cannot reach M0's on the grid: the reference method normalising by measured effect strength was narrower than M0 even at the lowest α in 20 of 21 RPE1 and all 21 non-library-target-set and development splits, so its coverage is reported at narrower-than-M0 width rather than as a width-matched gain (per-split α′, expected width, realised width ratio and matching status: Supplementary Table S2).

## Pre-registered confirmatory analysis (protocol v0.7)

All selections (model, k, primary method, hyper-parameters, the strongest baseline = Mdist-M, thresholds) were made on the development set and frozen with SHA-256 manifests before any confirmation-set response was read. Endpoints: **E1**, the difference in T5 coverage between a method and its comparator; **E2**, the coverage difference in a decision scenario (the 10% of test perturbations with the highest ĥ), always reported with the subset width ratio. Differences and 95% CIs come from paired generalised estimating equations with TSS-group clusters (*Interval estimation*, below). **B0** (failure present): M0 T5 coverage point estimate < 0.85 and upper 95% CI bound < 0.90. **Step 1** (M1 vs M0) passes if, for both E1 and E2, the lower CI bound is > 0 and the estimate ≥ 5 pp; **step 2** (M1 vs Mdist-M; hierarchical, tested only after step 1) uses a 3 pp margin. Guards: nominal marginal coverage within [0.88, 0.92]; M_perm mean E1 ≤ 2 pp with at most one permutation meeting the step-1 rule; ĥ predictability not in the pre-specified "P−" class. Classifications were taken verbatim from pre-written wording; the complete decision tables for both confirmation sets are given in Supplementary Table S3. Power at half the development effect was computed from n_E alone before responses were read.

### Statistical inference

**Interval estimation.** Let the analysis set be one (method, model, k, matching mode) combination. Each row is one *test unit*: one target gene in one split, so a gene contributes up to 21 rows (3 rounds × 7 folds, each gene a test unit once per round) and rows of the same gene are not independent. Write y_{gs} ∈ {0,1} for the coverage indicator of gene g in split s, and c(g) for the TSS group of g.

*Coverage per tier* (`analysis_v06.gee_tiers`). For each effect-strength quintile separately we fit an intercept-only GEE

g(E[y_{gs}]) = β₀, g = logit, marginal variance from the binomial family, working correlation = independence, clusters = TSS group c(g),

with `statsmodels.api.GEE(...,family=Binomial(),cov_struct=Independence())`. All rows of the same TSS group — including the same gene's rows across splits and different genes sharing a TSS group — are one cluster, so the sandwich (Liang–Zeger) covariance absorbs both the within-gene repetition and the within-TSS-group dependence. The reported coverage is expit(β̂₀) and the interval expit(β̂₀ ± 1.96·SE(β̂₀)) with the sandwich SE. The independence working correlation makes the point estimate numerically equal to the pooled mean, so only the interval depends on the GEE. When a tier is all-covered or none-covered (β̂₀ not finite), we report the pooled proportion with a cluster bootstrap interval instead (below) and flag the row as `method = "bootstrap (separation)"`.

*Paired coverage difference* (`analysis_v06.paired_T5`). For a method m versus the reference M0 in one tier, the two arms contain the *same* (split, gene) rows (asserted in code). We stack them, add an arm indicator m_i ∈ {0,1}, and fit the same logistic GEE with design [1, m] and the same clusters. The difference and its interval are obtained by the delta method on the two fitted probabilities p₁ = expit(β̂₀+β̂₁), p₀ = expit(β̂₀):

Δ̂ = p₁ − p₀, ∇ = [p₁(1−p₁) − p₀(1−p₀), p₁(1−p₁)], SE(Δ̂) = √(∇ᵀ V̂ ∇),

with V̂ the sandwich covariance of (β̂₀, β̂₁); the interval is Δ̂ ± 1.96·SE(Δ̂) and the two-sided p-value 2Φ(−|Δ̂|/SE). If either arm is all-covered or none-covered we refit with an identity link (`family=Gaussian()`), in which case Δ̂ = β̂₁ directly; such rows are flagged `how = "identity-link GEE (separation)"`. E1 is this quantity in the top quintile; E2 is the same quantity on the decision subset (P-2 below).

*Cluster bootstrap* (`analysis_v06.cluster_boot`). TSS groups are resampled with replacement (2,000 draws unless stated), the statistic is recomputed on each resampled frame, and the interval is the 2.5/97.5 percentiles. This is used for separated tiers, for the confounding analysis, and wherever a statistic is not a single coverage probability.

*What the interval covers.* These intervals quantify sampling variability of the coverage estimate over genes/TSS groups and splits. They are not conformal guarantees, and they are not valid for comparisons selected after inspecting the same rows; the pre-registered endpoints were fixed before the confirmation data were scored.

**Constructive reference (post-confirmation, descriptive).** If the score S is a strictly increasing function of the stratifying variable E (no proportionality needed) and units are exchangeable within a split, the split-conformal threshold at 1 − α is the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th calibration order statistic, so in the population limit a test unit is covered if and only if its E lies below the (1 − α) quantile. For tier j of K equal-count tiers the coverage is therefore clip(((1 − α) − (j − 1)/K)/(1/K), 0, 1); with α = 0.1 and K = 5 this is 1 for T1–T4 and 0.5 for T5. Finite calibration and test sets, split-specific references and calibration–test differences add variance without changing the centre, as verified by Monte Carlo with the actual sizes per split (`scripts/constructive_reference_mc.py`, log-normal E, seed 20261001, 4,000 replicates; output `results/constructive_reference_mc/mc_result.json`; Supplementary Table S4; an earlier draft's unarchived script differed by at most 0.003: 0.518, 0.511, 0.501 versus 0.517, 0.508, 0.504, affecting no conclusion). The mean-response model realises this case exactly because its score equals E_true. For every model we report its T5 coverage minus the constructive reference and the share R of the variance of log S not explained by log E_true (both defined post-confirmation as descriptive quantities).

**Descriptive analyses.** Tier-wise coverage for all methods and the three models, k = 20/200 and a split-half variant (tiers from half A, coverage from half B). Confounding (protocol v0.7 S5): logistic regression of M0 miscoverage on standardised log E_true, split-half reliability, log noise, log cell number, GO and STRING distance, baseline expression of the target in control cells, similarity and shared-neighbour strength; 95% CIs from TSS-group cluster bootstrap (500 resamples), replaced by Firth-penalised[10] bootstrap when ordinary fits separated. Logistic coefficients are non-collapsible and are interpreted for sign and residual association only.

**Selection of evaluation units.** Evaluation units require at least 30 cells; perturbations failing only this rule remain in training. Descriptively, among targets meeting all other inclusion rules, 77 of 1,863 (development), 350 of 2,101 (RPE1) and 89 of 5,772 (non-library target set) were excluded for cell number, and their bulk-level effect strength (computed with the same definition and a reference built from the included units) exceeded that of the included units (medians 7.85 vs 3.56, 6.60 vs 3.89 and 9.58 vs 3.22; Mann–Whitney p < 1e-36 in each dataset; 96%, 73% and 100% above the T5 cut-point of the included units), while measured effect strength decreased with cell number (Spearman −0.35, −0.64, −0.81; Supplementary Table S5; see *Limitations*).

**Functional enrichment (development set only; descriptive).** Target sets were compared against the background of all evaluation units of the screen with one-sided Fisher exact tests and Benjamini–Hochberg correction within each annotation source, reporting a representative non-redundant subset of terms. Sources: Gene Ontology (GAF and OBO of the same release) restricted to biological process and cellular component, and CORUM complexes. Three sets were examined: genes whose prediction sets failed to cover in the development audit, the same number of genes with the highest measured effect strength (as a control for the effect-strength composition of that set), and the top effect-strength tier. Complex-membership and network-degree comparisons of the top tier against the remaining units used Fisher exact tests and a logistic model containing both STRING degree and the number of GO annotations (descriptive only, as STRING's database channel includes complex annotations).

## Post-confirmation coupling test with GEARS (protocols gears_coupling v1.0 and v1.1)

*Evidence tier: post-confirmation.* This analysis was specified after both confirmations had been observed. It does not modify, redefine or replace any v0.7 configuration, criterion or result, and none of its conclusions is used as confirmatory evidence. Protocol v1.0 (sha256 `fb789a15c57f…`) was frozen on 2026-10-19 before GEARS was run; amendment v1.1 (sha256 `23b6f082e209…`), which adds three controls and changes nothing in v1.0, was frozen on 2026-10-28 after GEARS had finished on the server and the results had been downloaded, but before any GEARS output was unpacked or read locally. Both protocols are read-only files with hash manifests (`manifests/freeze_gears_coupling_v1.0.json`, `_v1.1.json`).

**Question.** Whether a non-linear model with a larger capacity, GEARS[3] (v0.1.2), reduces the coupling between the conformity score S1 and measured effect strength E_true that we observed for the linear models. The test is restricted to RPE1, the 21 frozen confirmation splits, the frozen score S1 (k = 50), the frozen per-split references μ_tr and σ_g, and α = 0.1.

**GEARS training.** Default GEARS settings (hidden size 64, 20 epochs, uncertainty off, batch size 32, test batch size 128), best epoch selected by GEARS on the validation fold (mean squared error on the top-20 differentially expressed genes), numpy seed 1 and torch seed 0 for every split, as in the benchmark script of Ahlmann-Eltze et al.[2]. Training data: single-cell expression of the RPE1 screen, transformed exactly as for the pseudobulks, with at most 16 cells per perturbation (uniform without replacement, seed 20270701) and all 11,485 non-targeting control cells, 8,749 readout genes; 1,751 E units and the 587 always-train units that are present in the GEARS GO graph (15 always-train units outside the graph cannot be represented by GEARS and were excluded from its training data). Each split's GEARS condition lists were derived from the frozen split files, with train, validation and calibration+test disjoint (asserted), and each split has its own data directory and its own co-expression graph built from that split's training perturbations only. For every split, GEARS predicted the 175 validation and 511–513 calibration+test perturbations; only the calibration+test predictions are used.

**Scoring (local).** The server produced only raw predictions. S1 of the GEARS prediction against the RPE1 pseudobulk was computed locally with the frozen score function, the split's stored reference and the stored calibration/test units; prediction rows and readout genes were asserted to be in the same order as the frozen caches. M0 coverage used the GEARS calibration scores of the same split.

**Quantities.** On the 5,253 test rows (21 splits pooled): CV = coefficient of variation of S1/E_true; R = 1 − R² of log S1 on log E_true (the share of the log score not explained by log effect strength); RER = 1 − mean(S1)/mean(E_true), the error reduction relative to the mean-response model, overall and within T5. GEARS versus linear-PCA was compared on the same (split, gene) rows, with 95% intervals from a TSS-group cluster bootstrap (2,000 resamples; seed 20261001), both arms resampled together.

**Decision rule (v1.0, written before running).** "Clearly reduced coupling" requires all of: (i) an accuracy precondition, RER of GEARS at least that of linear-PCA both overall and in T5; (ii) R_GEARS ≥ 2 R_PCA; (iii) a lower 95% bound of R_GEARS/R_PCA above 1.2; (iv) CV increased by at least 50%. "Coupling metrics up with accuracy down (not decoupling)" applies if R or CV reaches its threshold but (i) fails. "Not reduced" applies if the upper 95% bound of R_GEARS/R_PCA is below 1.5; any other outcome is "uncertain". The accuracy precondition was added because R and CV can also rise when a model only adds error unrelated to effect strength.

**Controls (v1.1).** All three are computed locally with the same score, references and units, and none changes the v1.0 verdict.

- *Blank:* the mean-response model, whose score equals E_true by construction (R = CV = RER = 0).
- *Positive (split-half):* each test perturbation's cells were split at random into halves A and B (the pre-existing split-half pseudobulks); the A-half pseudobulk is used as the "prediction" of the B-half pseudobulk, and all models are scored against the same B-half truth, with tiers taken from half A (so that tiering and evaluation use different cells). This predictor uses the perturbation's own measured cells (not deployable) to represent a predictor with all perturbation-specific signal, limited only by half-cell measurement noise. The verdict is "threshold reachable" if R_ctrl ≥ 2 R_PCA on the half-data basis and the control's RER exceeds linear-PCA's overall and in T5 (lower 95% bound of the paired difference > 0); "threshold unreachable" if R_ctrl < 2 R_PCA; "control fails" if the control is not more accurate than linear-PCA.
- *Negative (permuted predictions):* within each split, predictions were permuted among test perturbations by a derangement (every perturbation receives another perturbation's prediction; 20 permutations, seeds derived from 20261001 + 12000, the split and the permutation index); S1 is still computed with the top-50 genes chosen on the true response. Applied to linear-PCA (predictions recomputed with the frozen refit function and the split's training pool, matching the stored scores to < 1e-8) and to GEARS. The control passes if none of the 20 permutations is classified "clearly reduced coupling" by the v1.0 rule.

**Server execution.** The GEARS runs were executed by a separate agent on a rented GPU host (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 rather than the protocol-specified A800), using five runtime memory patches outside the restored, checksum-verified frozen code after initial out-of-memory failures; the final run executed all 21 splits once with no failed or debug output entering the reported results (see *Limitations* and Supplementary Note 2).

## Combinatorial perturbations (protocol combo_crosscell v1.2)

*Evidence tier: post-confirmation, pre-registered on previously unread data.* The protocol (`reports/protocol/combo_crosscell_v1.2.md`) was frozen on 2026-11-01 with a hash manifest (`manifests/freeze_v1.2.json`) at a time when no file of the dataset was present on the analysis machine. It reuses the frozen score, reference, embedding, fitting and conformal functions of protocol v0.7 unchanged.

**Data.** Norman et al.[9] combinatorial CRISPRa Perturb-seq in K562, as distributed by scPerturb[13] (`NormanWeissman2019_filtered.h5ad`, Zenodo record 13350497; MD5 verified). The file holds raw integer counts for 236 perturbations (105 single genes, 131 gene pairs) and 11,855 control cells. Because the file's `UMI_count` column holds guide UMIs, each cell's library size was recomputed as its total transcript count (identical to the file's `ncounts`). Pseudobulk profiles and split halves were computed as for the screens, by a chunked two-pass reader. Readout genes were those with a mean raw count of at least 0.1 in control cells (8,188), plus every target gene present in the file (8,234 in total); target-gene columns are excluded from every score, as before. Three target names were absent from the file (C19orf26, C3orf72, KIAA1804); the two pairs containing them were therefore not evaluated, leaving 129 evaluated pairs (median 301 cells, minimum 54).

**Task and splits.** The exchangeable unit is the unordered gene pair. Pairs were split by 3 rounds of grouped 7-fold cross-validation (21 splits; the same procedure as for the screens), with validation and calibration pairs drawn from the non-test pairs. All single-gene perturbations are always in the training pool, so both genes of every held-out pair have been seen alone ("unseen combination" task). Run-time assertions check that no held-out pair, and no response of one, enters the training pool, the reference or the models.

**Models.** (1) The mean-response model (the blank control; its score equals effect strength). (2) An additive baseline, ŷ_AB = y_A + y_B − y_ctrl, the sum of the two measured single-gene effects in log-normalised pseudobulk space[2, 9]. (3) Linear-PCA as for the screens, with a pair embedded as the sum of its two gene embeddings, P_AB = G[A] + G[B]; this extension to pairs is defined here, and K was selected on the validation pairs from {5, 10, 20, 30, 50} (K = 50 was chosen in all 21 splits).

**Quantities and decision rule.** Effect strength, tiers, the M0 split-conformal sets (α = 0.1) and R, the coefficient of variation of S1/E_true and the error reduction relative to the mean model were defined as for the screens; coverage intervals use an intercept-only binomial GEE clustered by pair, and all other intervals a pair-level cluster bootstrap (2,000 replicates). The rule was fixed in advance from the screens: with R_lo = 0.112 (the largest linear-PCA R on the three screens) and R_hi = 2R_lo = 0.224, R counts as clearly elevated when the error reduction has a lower confidence bound above zero, R ≥ R_hi, and the lower bound of R exceeds R_lo; as not elevated when the upper bound of R is below R_hi. Two negative controls were specified: effect strength permuted among the pairs of each split (20 derangements; stratified coverage and the between-model difference in R must vanish), and predictions permuted among test pairs (20 derangements; none may be judged clearly elevated). The split-half predictor served as positive control on the half-data basis.

**Further cell lines (HepG2, Jurkat).** Section 3.4 of the same protocol applies the confirmatory audit design to the essential-library CRISPRi screens of Nadig et al.[18]. Protocol amendment v1.2.1 (frozen before any of these data were read; `manifests/freeze_v1.2.1.json`) replaced the GEO source files, dense matrices too large to load on the analysis machine, by the scPerturb copies[13] (`NadigOConner2024_hepg2.h5ad`, `NadigOConner2024_jurkat.h5ad`; MD5 verified) and specified five checks before any analysis: raw integer counts (full scan), the number of cells and genes relative to the GEO files (identical in both cell lines), 2,393 targets and at least 1,000 control cells; all passed. Units, evaluation units, transcription-start-site groups (Ensembl 116 coordinates, merge distance 1 kb) and the 21 splits followed the confirmatory protocol, with unit metadata aggregated from the single-cell annotations. Models were the mean-response model and linear-PCA; the score, conformal procedure, tiers, statistics, controls and decision rule are those above, with transcription-start-site groups as bootstrap clusters. The pre-specified prediction was "R not elevated" for linear-PCA in both cell lines.

## Post hoc analyses of the combinatorial screen (protocol v1.3)

*Evidence tier: post hoc; specified after the v1.2 results had been observed, frozen with a hash manifest (`manifests/freeze_v1.3.json`) before computation.* **Gene-level resampling.** The 129 evaluated pairs are formed from 71 genes. On the v1.2 test predictions, without refitting, we resampled genes with replacement (2,000 replicates); a pair whose genes were drawn m_A and m_B times receives weight m_A·m_B (two-way, dyadic resampling; the vertex bootstrap for jointly exchangeable arrays[14], related to the pigeonhole bootstrap for crossed factors[15]) or, as a sensitivity variant defined for this analysis, (m_A + m_B)/2. All statistics are computed as weighted ratios. The v1.2 rule was re-applied with the two-way intervals; the official v1.2 verdict is unchanged.

**Unseen-component ablation.** R depends only on a model's score and the mean-model effect strength, not on the additive baseline; the additive baseline is undefined when a component has not been measured, so the comparable quantity in this setting is the R of linear-PCA, the model whose screen values define the thresholds. The 71 genes were split into five folds, three times (15 splits). For each fold, all single and double perturbations involving a held-out gene were removed from the training pool, reference and embedding; evaluated pairs involving a held-out gene were tested, separately for one and two held-out genes. K was fixed at 50 (the value selected in all v1.2 splits; K = 10 as sensitivity) because a validation set would further reduce the pool. For one held-out gene we also report a partial additive predictor (the measured single-gene component alone, equivalent to setting the unseen component's effect to zero). Conformal coverage was not computed, because the held-out pairs per fold are too few for a calibration set of the same kind. The interpretation rule (R upper bound below 0.224: back in the screen range; all v1.2 elevation conditions met: still elevated; otherwise uncertain; fewer than 40 distinct pairs: underpowered) was written before computation.
